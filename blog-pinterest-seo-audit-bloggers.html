Pinterest SEO Audit for Bloggers: Refresh Old Pins

A practical guide to auditing Pinterest SEO for a blog: how to inventory old pins, boards, and blog posts, how to find broken links, mismatched titles, and stale descriptions, how to fix and refresh the content worth keeping, and how to judge the result from your own numbers — with no invented statistics and no promised outcomes.

Quick answer: List every board, every pin that matters, and every blog post the pins lead to. Click the links, read each pin against its page, and record three kinds of problem: links that fail or lead to the wrong page, pins whose title, description, or image does not match the page, and boards whose contents have drifted from their titles. Fix the links first, then the match between pin and page, then the boards, and refresh the strongest old content with a new pin rather than deleting the old one. Judge the result from your own Pinterest analytics, compared like with like over a comparable period, and record the date of every change. This guide covers the audit and refresh only. For the wider picture of how bloggers use Pinterest, read our short guide to how bloggers use Pinterest to grow website traffic; for keyword research, read our guide to using search suggestions; for the board setup the audit will judge, read our board SEO guide; and for judging what happens after you publish, read our Pinterest analytics guide. Use the PinTrendFinder tool to organise the phrases the audit finds into a working keyword list.

Most bloggers treat Pinterest as a publishing channel and forget that it is also an archive. Every pin ever published stays published, unless it is changed or removed, and every board keeps accumulating pins, long after the blog post it led to has been updated, rewritten, or deleted. The result, after a year or two of steady publishing, is a profile that no longer describes the blog it is meant to serve. Some pins lead to pages that no longer exist. Some lead to pages that have changed so much that the pin's promise no longer matches them. Some carry titles written for a search phrase the blog no longer targets, or descriptions that mention a season, a price, or a product that has gone. Some boards contain a mixture of topics that made sense when the pins were added one by one, but that no longer read as a coherent collection to a visitor who opens the board. None of this happens because the blogger did anything wrong. It happens because publishing is visible and auditing is not, and because the work that gets done is the work that gets seen. An audit is the work that brings the archive back into line with the blog, and it is the cheapest traffic work a blogger can do, because it improves content that already exists, on a profile that already has history, instead of starting from nothing.

The audit in this guide is deliberately manual and deliberately slow. It does not depend on a paid tool, it does not promise a ranking improvement, and it does not claim any figure about Pinterest reach, clicks, or search behaviour, because no such figure can be stated honestly in general terms for every blog. What it does is give a blogger a method for looking at their own profile, their own pins, and their own numbers, and for making decisions from what they see. The method has four parts: inventory, diagnosis, repair, and measurement. The inventory is a list of what exists. The diagnosis is a judgement about each item on the list. The repair is the fix, in order of importance. The measurement is a comparison of the blogger's own numbers before and after, over a period long enough to mean something. A blogger who completes those four parts will know, at the end, what their Pinterest profile actually contains, which parts of it are working, which parts are broken, and what was changed and when. That knowledge, recorded, is worth more than any number of new pins published on top of a broken archive.

1. What an audit is, and what it is not. An audit is a review of what already exists, against a standard the blogger sets in advance. The standard in this guide is simple: every pin should lead to the page it promises, every pin's title and description should describe that page honestly, every board should contain pins that belong together under its title, and every blog post that Pinterest sends visitors to should be a post the blogger is still willing to stand behind. An audit is not a redesign of the whole profile, it is not a reason to delete everything and start again, and it is not a judgement on the blogger's taste or effort. It is a maintenance task, like checking the links on a website or updating a blog post that has gone out of date, and it should be approached in that spirit: methodically, without drama, and with a record of what was found and what was done. A blogger who approaches an audit as a chance to punish past decisions will delete good content and rewrite good titles. A blogger who approaches it as maintenance will keep what works, fix what is broken, and leave the rest alone.

An audit is also not a substitute for publishing. For bloggers starting from scratch, our Pinterest SEO beginners guide and our guide to growing on Pinterest cover the foundations this audit assumes. A profile that is audited but never refreshed will slowly go stale again, because the blog keeps changing, the seasons keep turning, and new content keeps arriving that belongs in boards that have not been reviewed. The audit and the publishing rhythm belong together, and the publishing rhythm is covered in our guide to how often to post on Pinterest. The audit sets the profile in order. The publishing rhythm keeps it in order. A blogger who audits once, publishes steadily, and audits again at the next season or the next year will have a profile that improves over time, because each audit starts from a better position than the last. A blogger who audits once and never again will have a profile that is tidy for a month and then drifts, because drift is what happens to any archive that is added to without review. The audit should therefore end with a date for the next audit, written down, in the same document as the findings, so that the next audit is a commitment rather than a good intention.

2. Before you start: gather the evidence in one document. An audit that is not written down is an audit that will be half-remembered and half-invented when it is acted on, and the whole point of the method is to replace impression with evidence. Start a single document — a spreadsheet, a table in a notes app, or a plain document — with the date of the audit at the top, and with columns for the board, the pin, the linked page, the problem found, the fix applied, and the date of the fix. Every pin that is checked gets a row. Every problem gets a note. Every fix gets a date. The document will be long, and that is a sign that it is working, because a profile that has been built over years contains a large number of items, and an audit that produces a short document has probably not looked at enough of them. Work through the document in a fixed order — boards first, then the pins on each board, then the blog posts the pins lead to — so that nothing is checked twice and nothing is missed, and so that the audit can be paused and resumed without losing the place.

Gather, alongside the document, the evidence the audit will judge from. The first piece of evidence is the profile itself, as a visitor sees it: the boards, their titles and descriptions, and the pins on each board, in the order Pinterest shows them. The second is the blog itself: the posts the pins lead to, as they stand today, including any post that has been rewritten, retitled, or removed since the pin was published. The third is the blogger's own Pinterest analytics, for a period the blogger chooses and records, showing, for the pins and boards under review, the measures the blogger will use to judge the refresh — saves, outbound clicks, and impressions, as shown in the blogger's own account, with no comparison to any other account and no claim about what those measures "should" be. Our analytics guide explains how to read those measures and how to avoid the common errors in interpreting them, and it should be read before the measurement stage of the audit. The fourth piece of evidence is the keyword list, which the audit will revise: the phrases the blogger is targeting, the phrases the search-suggestions guide helped collect, and the phrases the pins actually carry, compared against each other. The PinTrendFinder tool is the place to organise that list, and our guide to using Trends to find keywords is the place to check whether a seasonal phrase is rising or falling before a refresh is built around it.

3. The board audit: titles, descriptions, and drift. Start with the boards, because boards are the structure the pins hang from, and a structural problem affects every pin on the board. List every board, with its title and its description, exactly as shown, and ask three questions of each. Does the title name, plainly and in the words people search for, the topic the board contains? Does the description state, in a sentence or two, what the board contains and who it is for, in the same plain words as the title? And do the pins on the board belong together under that title, or have pins accumulated that belong on another board, or on no board at all? A board whose title promises one topic and whose pins cover three topics is a board that disappoints every visitor who opens it for the promised topic and finds the other two, and it is a board whose pins will be judged, by the visitor and by the platform, as a mixture rather than as a collection. The fix is usually not to rename the board, but to move the pins that do not belong, to a board where they do belong, or to a new board if a genuine new topic has grown large enough to hold one, following the guidance in our board SEO guide on when a new board is justified and when it is not.

Board drift has a characteristic pattern, and it is worth learning to recognise it, because it is the most common board problem and the easiest to miss. A board starts with a clear topic — say, beginner dinner recipes — and the first pins all belong to it. Over months, the blogger saves pins that are near the topic but not quite of it: a lunch recipe, a dessert, a kitchen tool, a recipe for a special occasion. Each addition, on its own, seems harmless, because each pin is related to the board in some way. Together, they turn a clear board into a general board, and a general board into a board that no visitor can describe in a sentence. The audit corrects drift by reading the board as a visitor would read it, from the title down through the first screen of pins, and asking whether the pins on that screen belong together. Pins that do not belong are moved, not deleted, because the pin itself may be good and the board may simply be the wrong home for it. A board that has drifted so far that it has no clear topic left should be split, or retired, with its pins moved to boards where they belong, and the decision should be recorded, with the reason, so that the next audit does not have to reconstruct it.

Two further board checks complete the board audit. The first is the board cover, which is the image a visitor sees for the board in the profile view, and which should be the strongest pin on the board, or an image made for the board that shows its topic clearly. Our guide to board covers covers the visual side in detail, and the audit should note any board whose cover does not show the board's topic, or whose cover is a pin that has since been moved to another board. The second is the number of boards itself. A profile with a very large number of boards, many of them holding only one or two pins, looks scattered, and a board with only one or two pins looks abandoned. The audit should note thin boards, and should consider whether each thin board's topic can be held by a larger board, or whether the thin board is a genuine new topic that needs a first batch of pins to become a real board, as the board SEO guide explains. Do not create boards for the sake of the audit, and do not delete boards for the sake of tidiness. Create or remove a board only where the pins, counted and read, justify it.

4. The pin audit: links, match, and staleness. The pin audit is the longest part of the work, and it should be done board by board, pin by pin, in the document started in section 2. For each pin, check the link first, because a pin that leads nowhere fails whatever its title and image promise. Click the pin, follow the link, and confirm that it opens the intended page, that the page still exists, that it loads without an error, and that it is the page the pin's title describes. Note every link that fails, every link that leads to a different page from the one intended — commonly because the blog post was moved, retitled, or merged — and every link that leads to a page that has been rewritten so thoroughly that the pin no longer describes it. A link that fails should be fixed at once, by editing the pin's link where the platform allows it, or by creating a corrected pin and removing the broken one where it does not, and the fix should be recorded with the date. A link that leads to the wrong page should be corrected to the intended page, or, if the intended page no longer exists and will not return, the pin should be removed or redirected, following the blog's own practice for removed posts, and the decision recorded.

Next, check the match between the pin and the page, which is the heart of Pinterest SEO for a blogger and the check most often skipped. Read the pin's title, read its description, look at its image, and then read the page it leads to, and ask whether the page delivers what the pin promises, in full, without stretching. A pin that promises a tutorial should lead to instructions, not to a list of ideas. A pin that promises a recipe should lead to the recipe, not to a round-up that mentions it. A pin that names a season, an audience, or a constraint — beginner, budget, quick, for a small space — should lead to a page that meets that season, audience, or constraint honestly, as the search-suggestions guide explains for the qualifiers in search phrases. A pin whose image shows a result the page does not show — a finished room, a finished dish, a finished craft — is a mismatch even if its title is accurate, because the visitor clicked on the image as well as on the words. Record every mismatch, with the pin and the page named, and fix the pin to match the page, or fix the page to match the pin, whichever is the honest correction. Do not fix a mismatch by changing the pin's words to describe a page that still does not deliver, and do not fix it by changing the page's title to match a pin that overpromises. The correction must be real, on one side or the other, or it is not a correction.

Staleness is the third pin check, and it is a matter of judgement rather than of failure. A pin is stale when its title, description, or image refers to something that has changed: a post that has been updated with new information, a product that is no longer available, a price that has changed, a season that has passed, a phrase that the blogger no longer targets, or an image style the blogger no longer uses. Stale pins are not broken, and they should not be treated as if they were. They should be refreshed, by rewriting the title and description to describe the page as it stands today, by replacing the image with one that shows the page's current content, or by creating a new pin for the updated page and letting the old pin stand as history, where the old pin still leads to a valid page and still earns saves. The choice between editing the old pin and creating a new one depends on what the platform allows, on whether the old pin has a history worth keeping, and on whether the page has changed enough to justify a new pin. Record the choice and the reason, because the next audit will need to know why two pins for the same page exist, or why an old pin's wording changed. A pin that is stale in a way that misleads — a price, a date, a claim that is no longer true — should be fixed before any other refresh work, because a misleading pin costs trust, and trust, once lost with a visitor, is not recovered by a later pin.

Duplicates deserve a note of their own, because they are common in a long-lived profile and because the instinct to remove them is often wrong. A blogger who has published several pins for the same blog post, each with a different title, image, or angle, has not duplicated the post. The blogger has given the same post several chances to be found, by different search phrases, in different seasons, and that is a legitimate strategy, covered in our short guide to how bloggers use Pinterest and in our guide to pin descriptions. The audit should distinguish between deliberate variations — different images, different titles, different angles, for the same page — and true duplicates, in which the same pin, with the same image and the same wording, appears more than once on the same board or across boards, with no difference that a searcher could notice. True duplicates should be consolidated: keep the version with the strongest history, remove the rest, and record the decision. Deliberate variations should be kept, but should be checked as a group, because a group of variations that all carry the same stale wording, or that all lead to a page that has changed, is a group that needs the same refresh, and the audit should note the group as one task rather than as several unrelated pins.

5. The blog-post audit: the page the pin promises. A Pinterest audit that stops at the pins is half an audit, because the visitor's experience ends on the blog post, and a pin that leads to a post that is itself out of date, broken, or mismatched will disappoint the visitor however good the pin is. For every blog post that Pinterest sends visitors to — starting with the posts whose pins were found to be broken or mismatched, and continuing to the posts that receive the most Pinterest visits according to the blogger's own analytics — read the post as a new visitor would read it, arriving from the pin, and ask whether it delivers the pin's promise, whether it is still accurate, and whether it still represents the blog. Check the post's title, its introduction, and its images, because those are what the visitor sees first, and check any link, download, or product mentioned in the post, because those are the parts most likely to have broken or changed since the pin was published. A post that has been updated should say so, with a date, so that a visitor who arrives from an old pin understands that the post has been revised. A post that has been removed should not be left as a dead end: it should redirect to the post that replaces it, or to a relevant post, following the blog's own practice, and every pin that led to it should be corrected to the new destination.

The blog-post audit should also check the post's own Pinterest readiness, because the pin is not the only way a visitor arrives from Pinterest, and because a post that is shared from the blog itself should carry the right information. Check that the post has an image that works as a pin, with alt text that describes it, and that the post's title, as shown on the page, matches the title the pins use, or differs from it for a stated reason. Our guide to pin design tips covers the image side, and our guide to rich pins setup covers the metadata that Pinterest reads from the page. The audit should note any post whose image is missing, broken, or unsuitable as a pin, and any post whose title on the page contradicts the titles of its pins, because that contradiction will confuse both the visitor and the searcher. Fix the post, or fix the pins, so that the two agree, and record which was changed. A post that cannot be fixed — a post that is so out of date that it should not be promoted at all — should have its pins removed or redirected, and the decision recorded, rather than being left to attract visitors to content the blogger no longer stands behind.

6. Prioritising the repairs: what to fix first, and what to leave. An audit of a long-lived profile will find more problems than can be fixed in one sitting, and the order in which they are fixed matters, because the most important fixes are not always the most visible ones. Fix first the problems that break the visitor's journey: links that fail, links that lead to the wrong page, and pins that mislead about price, date, or availability. Those are the problems that cost a visitor, and they should be fixed before any other refresh work, even if the fix is only to remove the pin or correct the link. Fix next the problems that affect the most visitors: the pins and posts that receive the most Pinterest visits according to the blogger's own analytics, and the boards that hold them, because a fix to a widely seen pin helps more visitors than a fix to a pin nobody sees. Fix next the mismatches between pin and page, starting with the posts whose pins promise a specific result, because a mismatch on a specific promise disappoints more sharply than a mismatch on a general one. Leave until last the cosmetic refreshes — new images for pins that already work, new wording for descriptions that are already accurate — because those are improvements, not repairs, and they should not displace the repairs that matter.

The prioritisation should be recorded, with a reason for each decision, because the audit will be resumed, and the person resuming it — commonly the same blogger, weeks later — will need to know why a particular pin was left, and whether it was left because it was good, because it was low priority, or because it was not yet checked. A simple marking system serves the purpose: a mark for broken, a mark for mismatch, a mark for stale, a mark for good, and a mark for not yet checked, applied to every row in the audit document. The marks turn a long document into a work list, and the work list turns the audit from a review into a plan. Work the list in order, fix the broken and the mismatched first, refresh the stale next, and leave the good alone. A pin marked good should not be rewritten for the sake of activity, because a good pin that is rewritten risks becoming a worse pin, and because the time spent rewriting good pins is time not spent fixing broken ones. The audit's discipline is to know when to stop as well as when to start, and to leave good content alone is part of that discipline.

7. Refreshing old content: new pins for old posts, done honestly. The refresh is the creative part of the audit, and it should be done after the repairs, not instead of them. A refresh takes a blog post that is still good — still accurate, still worth reading, still the post the blogger wants new visitors to find — and gives it a new pin, with a new title, a new description, and a new image, built around the phrase the post answers today, in the words people search for today. The phrase should come from the evidence the search-suggestions guide describes: the suggestions Pinterest itself shows for the topic, collected cleanly and dated, grouped by topic and intent, and chosen for fit and specificity rather than for popularity. The PinTrendFinder tool organises the phrases into a working list, and the list should be checked against the phrases the post's existing pins already carry, so that the new pin takes a phrase the post does not already serve, rather than repeating a phrase it already serves. A new pin that repeats an existing pin's phrase, with a slightly different image, adds little. A new pin that serves a new phrase, for a new angle on the same post, gives the post a new chance to be found.

The new pin's title should be a plain statement of the result or the answer, in the phrase's words, and its description should explain, in a sentence or two, what the post contains and who it is for, using the narrower phrases from the same topic group where they fit naturally. The image should show the result the phrase promises, following the pin design guidance, and it should be an image that honestly represents the post, not an image borrowed from another post or from a different result. The pin should be placed on the board that holds the post's topic, and it should be recorded in the audit document, with the phrase it carries, the date it was published, and the old pin, if any, that it refreshes, so that the next audit can see the relationship between the old and the new. Publish refreshed pins steadily, following the rhythm in our guide to how often to post, rather than publishing a large batch at once, because a steady rhythm is easier to sustain, easier to measure, and less likely to bury the blogger's other content. A refresh that is published steadily, with each pin recorded, is a refresh that can be judged. A refresh that is published in a burst, without a record, is a refresh that cannot be judged, however good the pins are.

Seasonality is the refresh's best friend and its most common trap, and the audit should treat it explicitly. A post that is seasonal — a Christmas recipe, a back-to-school guide, a summer garden post — should be refreshed in good time for its season, not after the season has begun, because a pin published after the season has started has missed the period in which searchers are planning. Our guide to using Trends to find keywords explains how to check whether a seasonal phrase is rising, and the audit should use that check before building a refresh around a seasonal phrase, so that the refresh is timed to the phrase's rise rather than to the blogger's convenience. At the same time, a seasonal pin that is refreshed out of season, with a title that names the season, will look stale to a visitor who finds it in the wrong month, and the audit should note seasonal pins whose titles or descriptions name a season that has passed, and should either refresh them for the coming season, at the right time, or leave them alone until then, rather than rewriting them into a form that suits no season. The record should show, for each seasonal pin, the season it serves and the date by which it should be refreshed, so that the next audit, or the seasonal check, finds the instruction rather than having to reconstruct it.

8. Measuring the refresh: your numbers, compared like with like. The measurement stage is where most Pinterest advice goes wrong, because it compares unlike with unlike, over too short a period, and draws a conclusion from a single pin. The measurement in this guide compares the blogger's own numbers, for a comparable period, before and after the refresh, allowing for season, and it judges a group of refreshed pins, not a single pin. Choose the measure before the refresh begins — saves, outbound clicks, or impressions, as shown in the blogger's own Pinterest analytics, with the choice recorded — and record the measure for the group of pins to be refreshed, for a period before the refresh, of a length the blogger chooses and records, such as the month before. Refresh the group, record the date of each refresh, and then record the same measure, for the same group, for a comparable period after the refresh, of the same length, allowing for any seasonal difference between the two periods. If the two periods fall in different seasons, the comparison should be treated with caution, and the seasonal effect should be stated, because a seasonal rise or fall will swamp the effect of the refresh, and a blogger who ignores it will attribute a seasonal change to the refresh, or will miss a real refresh effect hidden inside a seasonal fall.

A comparison group strengthens the measurement, and it costs nothing to create. Keep a second group of pins, similar in topic and age to the refreshed group, that is not refreshed during the measurement period, and record the same measure for that group over the same two periods. If the refreshed group improves and the comparison group does not, the refresh is a plausible cause, though not a proof, because other factors — a change in the platform, a change in the season, a change in the blog — may also have contributed. If both groups improve by a similar amount, the cause is more likely to be seasonal or platform-wide than the refresh, and the blogger should not claim the refresh as the cause. If the refreshed group does not improve, and the comparison group does not either, the refresh has not yet shown an effect, and the blogger should wait, check the pins again, and consider whether the refresh addressed the right problem — a link, a mismatch, or a phrase — before concluding that refreshing does not work. Our analytics guide explains the common errors in reading Pinterest numbers, including the error of judging a change from a single day's figures, and it should be read alongside this section. No figure in this guide states what a refresh "should" achieve, because no such figure can be stated honestly for every blog, and a blogger who is promised a figure is being promised something the promiser cannot know.

Record the measurement in the same document as the audit, with the dates, the periods, the groups, the measures, and the conclusion, stated cautiously. A conclusion such as "the refreshed group rose over the month after the refresh, while the comparison group did not, allowing for the seasonal rise in this topic" is an honest conclusion, and it is useful, because it tells the next audit what was tried and what happened. A conclusion such as "the refresh doubled traffic" is not honest, unless the numbers, compared like with like, actually show it, and unless the comparison allows for season and for the comparison group. The audit document, kept over several audits, becomes a record of what works for this blog, on this platform, for this audience, and that record is worth more than any general claim about Pinterest, because it is evidence about the blog itself. A blogger who keeps that record will make better decisions at each audit, because each decision will be informed by the last audit's evidence, rather than by a general rule that may not apply to this blog.

9. A worked example: one blog, one audit, in outline (illustrative). The example below is illustrative, with invented figures and invented timings, labelled as such, to show the shape of an audit, not to state what any real blog will find. The imaginary blog is a food blog with three years of Pinterest publishing, forty boards, and several hundred pins, whose owner has never audited the profile. The audit begins with a document, dated, and a board list. The board audit finds six boards whose pins have drifted: two dinner boards that have accumulated lunch and dessert pins, one baking board that contains kitchen tools, and three thin boards with two or three pins each. The drifted pins are moved to the boards where they belong, the thin boards are merged into larger boards, and the moves are recorded. The pin audit, board by board, finds a set of broken links, concentrated in pins published in the blog's first year, when the blog's addresses were structured differently; a set of mismatches, concentrated in pins whose posts were rewritten in the second year without the pins being updated; and a larger set of stale pins, whose descriptions name prices, seasons, or phrases that have changed. The broken links are fixed first, the mismatches next, and the stale pins are marked for refresh, in order of the visits their posts receive according to the blog's own analytics.

The refresh takes the ten posts that receive the most Pinterest visits, according to the blog's own numbers, and gives each a new pin, built around a phrase collected by the search-suggestions method, checked against the phrases the post's existing pins already carry, and published steadily over several weeks, each recorded with its phrase and date. A comparison group of ten similar posts is left unrefreshed. After a comparable period, the refreshed group's numbers, compared like with like, show an improvement that the comparison group does not show, allowing for the seasonal rise in the topic, and the owner records the result cautiously, with the dates and the periods. The audit ends with a date for the next audit, set for the following season, and with a list of the pins that were left, marked good, and the pins that were left because they were low priority. The example is invented, and no real audit will follow it exactly, but its shape — inventory, diagnosis, repair in order, refresh of the strongest content, measurement against a comparison group, and a dated commitment to the next audit — is the shape every real audit should follow, whatever it finds.

10. The ninety-minute audit: a starting point for a busy blogger. A full audit of a long-lived profile takes longer than an afternoon, and a blogger who waits for a free afternoon will never audit at all. The ninety-minute version below is a starting point, not a substitute, and it should be followed by the fuller audit, board by board, over the following weeks. Spend the first fifteen minutes listing the boards, with their titles, and marking any board whose title does not describe its pins. Spend the next thirty minutes clicking the links on the pins for the ten posts that matter most to the blog — the posts the blogger most wants new visitors to find — and fixing or recording every broken or wrong link. Spend the next twenty minutes reading those ten pins against their posts, and rewriting any title or description that does not match the post as it stands today. Spend the next fifteen minutes choosing one post to refresh, collecting its phrases by the search-suggestions method, and drafting the new pin's title and description. Spend the last ten minutes writing down what was found, what was fixed, what was left, and the date for the next session. Ninety minutes will not complete an audit, but it will complete the most important part of one, and it will give the blogger a document, a method, and a next date, which is more than a profile that has never been audited has ever had.

An audit, in the end, is an act of respect for the work already done. Every pin on a profile represents time spent — time writing the post, making the image, choosing the words — and an audit honours that time by making sure the pin still leads somewhere worth going, still says what it means, and still sits in a collection that makes sense. A blogger who audits will find that much of the old work is still good, that some of it needs a small fix, and that a little of it should be retired, and the blogger will end the audit with a profile that is smaller in the right places, clearer in every place, and ready for the new pins that will be added to it. Start with the links, continue with the match, refresh the strongest old content, measure from your own numbers, and write the next audit's date in the document before the document is closed. For the keyword research the refresh depends on, use our guide to search suggestions and the PinTrendFinder tool; for the boards the audit judges, our board SEO guide and our guide to board covers; for the rhythm of publishing after the audit, our guide to how often to post; and for the measurement, our analytics guide. The archive is already there. The audit is what makes it work again.

Frequently asked questions

How often should a blogger audit Pinterest SEO?

A full audit once or twice a year suits most bloggers, with a shorter check each season for seasonal content. A blog that publishes weekly, or that depends heavily on Pinterest traffic, benefits from the more frequent rhythm, because titles, links, and seasonal relevance drift faster when new content is added often. A blog that publishes rarely can audit once a year and still stay in good order. The audit should be dated and recorded, so that the next audit starts from what the last one found, rather than from scratch.

What should I check first in a Pinterest SEO audit?

Check the links first, because a pin that leads nowhere, or to the wrong page, fails whatever its title and image promise. Click a sample of pins from each board, and every pin for your most important posts, and confirm that each link opens the intended page, that the page still exists, and that the page still answers the promise in the pin. Next, check the match between pin and page: the title, the description, and the image should describe the page the visitor reaches. A pin whose link works but whose promise the page does not keep is the second most common audit finding, and it costs the visitor's trust as well as the click.

Should I delete old pins that no longer perform?

Usually no. Deleting a pin removes whatever history, saves, and links it has accumulated, and a pin that performs poorly today may still be found by a searcher tomorrow, or may lead to a page that has since been improved. The audit approach is to fix first: correct the link, rewrite the title and description to match the page, replace a weak image, or move the pin to a better-fitting board. Delete only pins that are broken beyond repair, that lead to pages that no longer exist and will not return, that promise something the blog no longer offers, or that mislead the visitor. Record every deletion, with the reason, so that the decision can be revisited.

How do I know if a refreshed pin worked?

Judge a refreshed pin by your own numbers, compared like with like, over a period long enough for the change to be seen. Compare the same measure — saves, outbound clicks, or impressions, as shown in your own Pinterest analytics — for a comparable period before and after the refresh, allowing for season, because a seasonal pin will rise and fall with the season regardless of the refresh. One pin, watched for a few days, proves nothing. A group of refreshed pins, watched over a month, compared with a group that was not refreshed, shows a pattern, and the pattern, not any single pin, is the evidence. Record the date of each refresh, so that the comparison is honest.

Can I audit Pinterest SEO without a paid tool?

Yes. The audit in this guide uses only the Pinterest search box, your own boards and pins, your own blog, and your own Pinterest analytics, together with the free PinTrendFinder tool for organising keywords. No paid tool is needed to click links, to read titles and descriptions, to compare a pin with its page, or to record what was found. A paid tool can speed up a large audit, and it can surface data a manual audit cannot, but it does not replace the judgement at the centre of the audit: whether the pin, the board, and the page match, and whether the visitor who clicks will find what was promised.
