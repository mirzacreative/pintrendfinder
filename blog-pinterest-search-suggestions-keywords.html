Pinterest Search Suggestions for Keyword Research

A practical guide to using Pinterest search suggestions for keyword research: what autocomplete, guided search, and related searches each reveal, how to collect suggestions without guessing, how to turn a raw list into board and pin keywords, and how to prioritise the phrases worth building content around — in the words people actually search for, with no invented numbers and no promised outcomes.

Quick answer: Type a seed phrase into the Pinterest search box and write down every autocomplete suggestion exactly as shown. Run the search, note the guided words under the search box and the related searches shown with the results, then click a suggestion and repeat. Group the phrases you collect by topic and intent, use the broadest plain phrase for the board title, the narrower phrases for pin titles and descriptions, and the most specific phrases for single pins that answer one question. Prioritise fit and specificity over popularity: a phrase you can answer well, with content you can make honestly, is worth more than a broad phrase you cannot answer. This guide covers the suggestion method only. Keyword research on Pinterest is often taught backwards. A creator is told to brainstorm keywords, to invent phrases that sound like searches, and then to hope that people search in those words. The suggestion method reverses that order. It starts with what people already search for, as Pinterest itself reports it, in the search box, and it asks the creator to listen before writing. The suggestions Pinterest shows while you type are not guesses. They are phrases drawn from real searches and saves on the platform, offered in the order Pinterest judges most relevant to what you have typed so far, and they are phrased in the audience's words, including the qualifiers the audience adds — beginner, small, easy, budget, for a named room, for a named season — that a creator brainstorming alone would often miss. A suggestion list is therefore a record of demand, in the audience's language, and it is available to anyone with a Pinterest account, without a paid tool, in a few minutes. The skill is not in finding the suggestions. It is in collecting them cleanly, grouping them honestly, and turning them into boards and pins that answer the phrase, rather than borrowing the phrase for content that does not.

1. The three kinds of suggestion, and what each one tells you. Pinterest offers suggestions in three places, and each place tells you something different. Autocomplete is the list that appears while you type, before you press enter. It shows completions of the phrase you have started, in the words people use to finish it, and it is the closest record of how a search begins: the seed phrase plus the qualifier the searcher adds. Guided search is the row of words Pinterest shows under the search box after a search has run, which you can click to narrow the search. It shows the dimensions people use to refine a topic — a material, a style, a room, a season, a skill level — and it is the closest record of how a broad topic breaks into narrower interests. Related searches are the phrases shown alongside or below the results, which suggest neighbouring topics the searcher may also want. They show the topics that sit next to your topic in the audience's mind, and they are useful for finding the next board, the next pin series, or the next angle on a topic you already cover. A complete collection uses all three. Autocomplete alone gives you completions of one phrase. Guided search alone gives you refinements of one result set. Related searches alone give you neighbours without the centre. Together, they give you a map: the centre phrase, its completions, its refinements, and its neighbours.

The three kinds of suggestion also differ in stability. Autocomplete changes as you type each letter, and it changes over time as searches change, so a suggestion recorded today may not appear next month, and a suggestion that appears for one spelling may not appear for another. Guided words change with the results, and they reflect the pins Pinterest has for the topic, so a guided word that appears for a topic with many pins may not appear for a topic with few. Related searches change with the season and with what is being saved. None of that makes suggestions unreliable. It makes them a snapshot, and a snapshot should be dated. Record the date with every collection, repeat the collection when you revisit a topic, and treat a suggestion that appears consistently, across several collections, as stronger evidence than a suggestion that appears once. The method in this guide is built around that discipline: collect, date, group, and re-check, rather than collecting once and treating the list as permanent.

2. Collecting suggestions cleanly: the seed, the slow type, and the exact wording. Start with one seed phrase, not with a list. A seed phrase is a short, plain phrase that names your topic in the audience's words: two or three words, without a qualifier, such as the name of a room, a craft, a recipe type, or a problem. Type the seed slowly into the search box, one word at a time, and write down every autocomplete suggestion at each stage, exactly as shown, without correcting the spelling, without shortening the phrase, and without adding your own words. The exact wording matters, because the suggestion is evidence of how people phrase the search, and editing it destroys the evidence. If a suggestion is a phrase you would never write, record it anyway, and judge it later, at the prioritising stage, rather than discarding it during collection. A suggestion that sounds awkward to you may be the phrase a large number of searchers use, and a phrase you prefer may be one nobody searches for. Collection is for recording. Judgement comes later.

Run the search, and record the guided words and the related searches shown with the results, again exactly as shown. Then click one suggestion — the one that looks most relevant to your content — and repeat the process: note the autocomplete suggestions for that longer phrase, note the new guided words, and note the new related searches. Each click is a step deeper into the topic, and each step reveals a narrower set of phrases, which is how a broad topic becomes a set of specific pin ideas. Work in a single document, with the seed phrase at the top, the date, and each suggestion on its own line, grouped by the step at which it was found. Do not collect in a notebook you will lose, and do not collect in your head. A keyword list that is not written down is a list that will be half-remembered and half-invented when it is used, and the whole point of the suggestion method is to replace invention with evidence. Repeat the process for each seed phrase, one at a time, and remove duplicates only at the end, keeping a note of how many seed phrases produced each suggestion, because a phrase that appears from several seeds is a phrase with broader demand.

Collect in a clean state where you can. Suggestions can be influenced by your own account history, by pins you have saved, and by the language and location settings of the account, so the suggestions you see may not be exactly the suggestions a new visitor sees. That does not make the collection useless. It makes it a collection from one viewpoint, and the viewpoint should be noted: the account used, the language, and the date. Where possible, compare the suggestions from a second viewpoint — a different account, a browser without a sign-in, or a colleague's search — and note where the lists agree and where they differ. Phrases that appear in both lists are the strongest evidence. Phrases that appear in only one list should be checked before they are built around. And be careful with personalisation in the other direction: a suggestion that appears because you searched for it yesterday is not evidence of demand. It is evidence of your own history. A dated, written collection, compared across viewpoints, is the guard against that error.

3. Grouping the raw list: topic, intent, and the qualifier that does the work. A raw suggestion list is a long list of phrases, and it becomes useful only when it is grouped. Group first by topic: which phrases belong to the same subject, which belong to a neighbouring subject, and which belong to a different subject altogether. A topic group should be narrow enough that one board could hold it, and broad enough that it is not a single pin. Group next by intent: what is the searcher trying to do? A searcher may be looking for ideas to save, for instructions to follow, for a product to buy, or for a problem to solve, and the qualifier in the phrase usually names the intent: ideas, tutorial, how to, easy, beginner, budget, for a named room, for a named event. Group the phrases by that qualifier, because the qualifier decides what kind of pin and what kind of linked page the phrase needs. A phrase ending in ideas needs a pin that leads to a collection or a round-up. A phrase ending in how to needs a pin that leads to instructions. A phrase ending in a product need needs a pin that leads to a product or a shop page. A phrase whose intent you cannot name is a phrase you should not build around yet, because you do not yet know what the searcher wants.

The qualifier is often the most informative word in the phrase, and it deserves a close reading. A qualifier that names an audience — beginner, for kids, for small spaces — tells you who the pin is for, and the pin and the page should speak to that audience, in that audience's terms. A qualifier that names a constraint — budget, easy, quick, no sew, gluten free — tells you what the searcher will not accept, and the pin should honour the constraint honestly, not borrow the word for content that does not meet it. A qualifier that names a season or an event — Christmas, Halloween, wedding, back to school — tells you when the phrase matters, and the pin should be published in good time for that season, not after it. A qualifier that names a style — modern, farmhouse, minimalist — tells you what the searcher pictures, and the pin image should show that style, not a different one. Read each qualifier as a requirement, not as decoration, and discard any phrase whose qualifier you cannot honestly meet. A board or pin that borrows a qualifier it does not meet will disappoint the visitor who clicks, and the disappointment will cost more than the click was worth.

4. Turning suggestions into board keywords. A board keyword is a broad phrase that names a topic plainly, in the words people search for, and that can hold many pins. Choose it from the topic groups you made, by asking which phrase names the whole group, which phrase a visitor would expect to see as a board title, and which phrase the pins in the group all belong under. The board title should be that phrase, or a short, plain form of it, with the qualifier that defines the board where one is needed: a board for small bedrooms is clearer as "Small Bedroom Ideas" than as "Bedrooms", and a board for beginner recipes is clearer as "Beginner Dinner Recipes" than as "Recipes". The board description should state, in a sentence or two, what the board contains and who it is for, in the same plain words as the title, and it should carry the narrower phrases from the group naturally, as a description of the contents, not as a list of keywords. Our board SEO guide covers the title, description, and setup in detail, and our guide to board covers covers the visual side. The suggestion method supplies the words. The board guides supply the setup.

Do not make a board for every suggestion. A board for a phrase with only one or two pins looks abandoned, and a profile with dozens of near-identical boards looks scattered. Make a board for a topic group that can hold a growing collection, and use the narrower phrases inside the group as pin titles, not as board titles. If a topic group is large, split it by the qualifier that divides it most cleanly — by room, by season, by skill level — and make one board for each split, with a title that names the split plainly. If a topic group is small, keep it as one board, and let the pin titles carry the narrow phrases. And do not make a board for a phrase you cannot fill. A board should be started with a first batch of pins, as the board SEO guide explains, and the cover should be designed from the strongest pin on the board, rather than for a board that does not yet exist. The suggestion list tells you what people search for. The board tells them you have a collection worth opening. The two must match.

5. Turning suggestions into pin keywords. A pin keyword is a narrower phrase that the pin's page actually answers, and it belongs in the pin title and description, in the words of the suggestion, where it fits naturally. Choose it by asking which phrase the linked page answers, in full, without stretching. A pin that leads to a page about one recipe should carry the phrase for that recipe, not the phrase for a whole cuisine. A pin that leads to a tutorial should carry the phrase that names the skill taught, not a broader phrase that promises more than the tutorial delivers. The pin title should be a plain statement of the result or the answer, in the suggestion's words, and the pin description should explain, in a sentence or two, what the page contains and who it is for, using the narrower phrases from the same topic group where they fit. The pin image should show the result the phrase promises, as our guide to pin design tips and our guide to pin descriptions explain. A pin keyword borrowed from a suggestion list, placed on a pin that does not deliver it, is not keyword research. It is a mismatch, and the visitor who clicks will leave.

Use one main phrase per pin, and let the supporting phrases from the same group appear naturally in the description, rather than stuffing the title or the description with every phrase from the list. A title that carries three phrases will read as noise, and a description that lists phrases without sentences will read as a list, not as an explanation. A single pin can serve one phrase well, and a series of pins can serve a topic group well, with each pin taking one narrow phrase and one clear answer. That series is the practical output of the suggestion method: a topic group becomes a board, and the narrow phrases in the group become a series of pins, each with its own title, image, and page, published steadily, as our guide to how often to post on Pinterest explains for the publishing rhythm. Keep a record of which phrase each pin carries, in the same document as the suggestion collection, so that the next pin in the series takes the next phrase, and no phrase is used twice for two pins that answer the same question, and no phrase is left unused because it was forgotten.

6. Prioritising: fit, specificity, and the results check. Prioritise the raw list in three passes. The first pass is fit: can you answer this phrase well, with content you already have or can make honestly, for the audience the qualifier names, meeting the constraint the qualifier states? A phrase you cannot answer should be set aside, however popular it looks, because a pin that does not answer its phrase will not help the visitor or the account. The second pass is specificity: a clear, narrow phrase that names a result, an audience, or a problem usually brings a better-matched visitor than a broad phrase that could mean many things, and a narrow phrase is also easier to answer well, because the page can be focused on one question. The third pass is the results check: search the phrase, look at the top results, and ask whether the phrase is already answered well, whether the results are thin or mismatched, and what angle is missing. If the top pins already answer the phrase thoroughly, you need a clearer angle — a different audience, a different constraint, a different format — or a different phrase. If the results are thin, outdated, or mismatched to the phrase, the phrase may be a genuine opening, and a clear, honest pin may serve it well.

Do not prioritise by the order Pinterest shows the suggestions, and do not treat the first suggestion as the most valuable. The order reflects relevance to what you typed, in the moment, from one viewpoint, and it is not a measure of demand, of competition, or of value to your account. Do not prioritise by length either: a long phrase is not necessarily a better phrase, and a short phrase is not necessarily a worse one. A long phrase that names a very specific need, for a very small audience, may be worth one pin. A short phrase that names a broad topic, for a large audience, may be worth a board and a series. The prioritising question is always the same: which phrase, answered well, brings a visitor who will find what they wanted, save the pin, and click to a page that delivers it? Answer that question phrase by phrase, in the document, with a note for each phrase kept, set aside, or used, and the prioritised list will be a working plan, not a pile of phrases. For the wider demand picture, cross-check the top phrases with our Trends keywords guide, which shows how a phrase behaves over time, and keep the two methods separate: suggestions show what people search for now, in their words; Trends shows how interest moves over time.

7. Combining suggestions with Trends and with your own content. Suggestions and Trends answer different questions, and they are strongest together. Suggestions answer the question, "In what words do people search for this topic?" Trends answers the question, "When does interest in this topic rise and fall, and which related topics are rising?" Use suggestions to phrase the board and the pin, and use Trends to time the publishing and to choose between two phrases that both fit. A phrase that is seasonal should be published before its season, as the Trends guide explains, and a phrase that is rising should be prioritised over a phrase that is falling, where both fit equally well. Neither method, alone, decides what to publish. The third input is your own content: what you can make well, what you already have, and what your audience already responds to. A phrase that fits the suggestions, that is rising on Trends, and that you can answer better than the current results is the strongest possible candidate. A phrase that fits only one of the three should be treated with caution. A phrase that fits none of the three should not be built around, however attractive it sounds in a brainstorm.

Your own analytics are the check on both methods, over time. After publishing pins for the top phrases, use our analytics guide to look at which boards earn profile visits, saves, and outbound clicks, and whether the boards and pins built from suggestion phrases behave differently from the boards and pins built another way. Do not judge a phrase on a single week's numbers, because analytics moves for many reasons, and a keyword is one variable among many. Look for a pattern over a month, across several pins, before deciding that a phrase works or does not. And keep the record: the suggestion collection, the date, the phrases chosen, the pins built, and the pattern observed, in one document, so that the next round of research starts from evidence, not from a feeling. The suggestion method is a loop, not a single exercise: collect, group, build, measure, and collect again, with the list improving each time it is used.

8. The workflow: a repeatable suggestion session. Run the suggestion session as a batch, for one topic at a time, in about an hour. Start with the seed phrases: three to five seeds for the topic, each two or three words, in the audience's language. Collect the suggestions for each seed, slowly, exactly as shown, with the date and the viewpoint noted, and record the guided words and related searches at each step. Group the raw list by topic and by intent, reading the qualifier in each phrase as a requirement. Choose the board phrase for each topic group, and the pin phrases for the pins in the group, and write the board title and the pin titles in the document, in the suggestion's words, before any pin is designed. Check each phrase against the results, and set aside any phrase you cannot answer honestly, or that is already answered better than you can answer it. Cross-check the top phrases with Trends, for timing and for direction, and prioritise by fit, specificity, and the results check, in that order. Then build: the board first, with a first batch of pins, then the pin series, one phrase per pin, published steadily, with the record kept as you go.

Keep the suggestion document as a living record. When a new pin is published, mark the phrase used, with the pin and the date. When a phrase is set aside, mark the reason, so that it is not collected again and judged again from scratch. When a season approaches, re-run the collection for the seasonal seeds, and compare the new list with the old, noting which phrases have appeared, which have disappeared, and which qualifiers have changed. When a new topic is started, run a fresh session, rather than reusing the list from an old topic, because a list built for one topic will not phrase another. And review the whole document every few months, removing phrases that no longer fit the account, adding phrases from new collections, and checking that every board on the profile still carries a phrase from a current collection, or a phrase that has been tested and kept. A keyword list that is never reviewed will drift from what people search for, and a board that drifts from its phrase will drift from its visitors. The session is short. The record is what makes it last.

9. Mistakes that waste a suggestion list. The most common mistakes with suggestions are all versions of the same error: treating a suggestion as a promise, rather than as evidence to be checked. Collecting only the first suggestion, and building a whole board around it, wastes the map the other suggestions provide. Editing the wording during collection destroys the evidence, and the edited phrase may be one nobody searches for. Making a board for every suggestion creates a scattered profile of thin boards, and stuffing every phrase into one pin title creates a pin that promises everything and delivers nothing. Borrowing a qualifier the content does not meet — beginner, budget, easy, for a named audience — disappoints the visitor who clicks, and teaches the visitor not to trust the next pin. Ignoring the results check means building a pin for a phrase that is already answered well, with no clearer angle, and ignoring Trends means publishing a seasonal phrase after its season. Judging a phrase on one week's analytics means changing a working pin for a reason that is not a reason. And collecting once, and never re-running the session, means working from a snapshot that grows older every month, while the searches it recorded move on. Each mistake is fixed by the same discipline: collect exactly, group honestly, build only what you can answer, check the results, time with Trends, measure over a month, and re-collect.

10. Accessibility, honesty, and the limits of suggestions. A suggestion list should be used honestly, and its limits should be stated plainly. Suggestions show what people search for, in the words they use, from one viewpoint, on one date. They do not show how many people search, how often, or with what outcome, and no number should be invented from a suggestion list. A phrase that appears first is not necessarily searched most, a phrase that appears often is not necessarily searched often, and a phrase that does not appear is not necessarily never searched. Suggestions are a starting list, not a ranking, and they should not be presented, to a client or to a reader, as a measure of demand. Use them to phrase content that answers a real question, for a real audience, and let analytics, over time, show which phrases bring matched visitors. A suggestion used honestly — a phrase answered well, a qualifier met, a page that delivers what the pin promised — is a useful tool. A suggestion used as a label for content that does not answer it is a broken promise, and it should not be used that way, however attractive the phrase looks in the search box.

Finally, keep suggestions in their place. They are one input to keyword research, alongside Trends, alongside your own content, and alongside the analytics that show what actually happened. They will not, on their own, bring traffic, sales, or followers, and no guide should promise that they will. What they do, reliably, is put the audience's words in front of you, before you write, so that the board title, the pin title, and the description can be phrased in the words the audience already uses, rather than in the words you would have guessed. Start with one seed phrase, collect slowly, group honestly, and build one board and one pin series from the list, with the record kept. Frequently asked questions

What are Pinterest search suggestions?

Pinterest search suggestions are the phrases Pinterest offers while you type a search, the guided words it shows under the search box after a search, and the related searches it shows alongside results. They are drawn from what people actually search for and save on Pinterest, in the words those people use, so they are a direct record of demand, phrased by the audience rather than by the creator. Use them as a starting list for keyword research, not as a final decision, and check each suggestion against your own content before you build a board or a pin around it.

How do I collect Pinterest search suggestions for keyword research?

Repeat for each seed phrase, remove duplicates, and you will have a raw list of real search phrases to prioritise.

How do I turn search suggestions into board and pin keywords?

A board should carry a broad phrase that people search for, a pin should carry a narrower phrase that the pin's page actually answers, and the wording on the board, the pin, and the linked page should match, so that a visitor who clicks finds what the phrase promised.

How do I prioritise Pinterest keyword suggestions?
