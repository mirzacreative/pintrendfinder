How to Use Pinterest Trends to Find Keywords

A practical guide to Pinterest Trends: where to find the tool, how to read the trend graph, how to compare keywords, how to spot seasonal peaks early — and how to turn a trending search term into pins that actually rank.

Quick answer: Open Pinterest Trends, type a term from your niche, and read the graph: a rising line means demand is growing, a flat line means steady interest, and a sharp peak means a seasonal moment. Compare two or three similar terms, note the related terms Pinterest suggests, then build pins around the strongest keyword — putting it in the pin title, the first sentence of the description, and the destination page. Publish seasonal content 30 to 45 days before the peak, not at it.

Most Pinterest creators choose keywords by guessing. They write a pin title that sounds good to them, publish it, and wonder why it gets no impressions. Pinterest Trends removes the guessing. It is Pinterest's own free tool, built on the platform's real search data, and it shows you what people are actually searching for, whether interest in a topic is rising or falling, and when the seasonal peaks happen. A keyword that is trending is a keyword with an audience already waiting. This guide shows how to use the tool step by step, how to read what it tells you, and how to turn a trend into a pin that ranks — including the mistakes that make Trends data useless if you read it the wrong way.

If you are new to Pinterest SEO, start with our Pinterest SEO checklist for beginners — it covers profile setup, Rich Pins and the basics this guide builds on. This guide is about one skill inside that setup: finding keywords from real Pinterest search data.

1. What Pinterest Trends is — and where to find it. Pinterest Trends is a free tool from Pinterest that shows search interest for a term over time. You can find it at trends.pinterest.com, or from Pinterest's business hub under the Trends section. Type any term — "small bedroom ideas", "easy weeknight dinner", "fall wedding guest dress" — and you get a graph of search interest over the past year, a list of related terms people also search for, and example pins that are popular for that topic. You can filter by country, by age group and by gender, so you can check whether a trend is global or specific to your audience. The tool is free and needs no advertising account. It is the only keyword data source that comes directly from Pinterest itself, which makes it the most reliable starting point for any keyword decision on the platform.

2. How to read the trend graph — the only skill that matters. The graph shows relative search interest, not absolute numbers. A value of 100 is the highest point for that term in the selected period; a value of 50 means half as many searches as the peak. That means you cannot compare the height of two different graphs and conclude one term is bigger than the other — you can only compare the shape. What matters is the direction and the pattern. A line that climbs steadily over months means a growing topic worth building content around. A line that is flat and stable means steady, reliable demand — a good evergreen keyword. A sharp spike followed by a fall means a moment or a viral trend: useful if you can publish fast, dangerous if you build your whole strategy on it. And a line with a regular wave that peaks at the same time every year means a seasonal topic. Look at the shape first, and only then look at the numbers. A term with a rising line and moderate interest beats a term with a huge spike that is already falling.

3. Compare terms before you commit to one. Pinterest Trends lets you add more than one term to the same graph, and this comparison is where the tool earns its keep. Two phrases can mean almost the same thing and have very different search demand: "small bedroom ideas" and "tiny bedroom decor" might look interchangeable to you, but one may have twice the interest of the other, or one may be rising while the other fades. Type your top two or three candidate phrases into the comparison view and let the shapes decide. Pick the term with the stronger, steadier or faster-rising line — not the one you personally prefer. Then check the related terms Pinterest lists below the graph: these are phrases people search for alongside your term, and they are your best source of secondary keywords. A related term with a rising graph of its own is often a better target than the main term, because it has real demand and less competition. Use the free PinTrendFinder tool alongside Trends to cross-check the exact phrases people type, so your final keyword is backed by two sources instead of one.

4. Spotting seasonal peaks — and publishing before them. Pinterest is a planning platform, not a news platform. People search for Christmas ideas in October, for Halloween costumes in August, and for summer recipes in May. The trend graph shows this clearly: seasonal terms start rising 30 to 45 days before the event and peak a week or two before it. That lead time is your advantage — if you use it. A pin published at the peak of a seasonal trend is already too late: Pinterest needs time to test a new pin and distribute it, and by the time it ranks, the searches have moved on. The rule is simple: find the date the line starts rising on the graph, and publish before that date. For Christmas content, that means October. For back-to-school content, July. For wedding season, late winter. Check the graph for last year's peak date too — it usually repeats within a week or two — and set a reminder to publish your seasonal pins a month ahead of it. One well-timed seasonal pin can bring more traffic than a dozen off-season ones.

5. From a trending term to a pin that ranks. Finding a keyword is only half the job; the pin still has to use it properly. Put the exact keyword in the pin title, in the first sentence of the description, and on the page the pin links to — title, description and destination page together are what Pinterest reads to decide what a pin is about. Write the description the way our guide to Pinterest pin descriptions that rank explains: keyword first, benefit second, one call to action, a few relevant hashtags. Save the pin to a board whose name matches the keyword, and create two or three different pin designs for the same page so Pinterest has more than one version to test. Then watch: give a new pin two to three weeks before judging it, because Pinterest distributes fresh pins gradually. If impressions climb, make more pins on related terms from the same trend. If they stay flat after three weeks, the keyword may be too competitive for a new account, and a narrower related term is the better next target.

6. Trending topics, not just trending keywords. Pinterest Trends also has a browse view that shows what is trending right now across the whole platform, split by category — home, food, fashion, beauty, travel and more. This is useful in a different way from searching a single term: it shows you topics you would never have thought to check. Spend ten minutes a week scrolling the trending topics in your category and note anything that overlaps with your niche. A trending topic that is close to what you already write about is a fast content opportunity: a new pin, a new blog post, or a new angle on an existing post. But do not chase every trend. A trend that has nothing to do with your niche brings the wrong audience — people who click, do not save, and never come back. Pinterest reads that behaviour, and it hurts your account more than the extra impressions help. Take only the trends that fit what your account is already about.

7. Mistakes that make Pinterest Trends useless. The first mistake is reading absolute numbers into a relative graph — a peak of 100 for a small niche term does not mean it is bigger than a peak of 60 for a huge one. The second is chasing a spike that has already peaked: if the line is falling fast, the moment is over, and a pin published now will arrive after the audience has left. The third is ignoring the filters: a term that trends in the United States may be flat in the United Kingdom, and a term popular with one age group may be invisible to another — check the filters match your audience before you build content around a trend. The fourth is using Trends once and never again: trends change monthly, and a keyword that was strong in spring may be weak by autumn. The fifth is finding a great keyword and then not using it anywhere — a trending term only helps if it appears in your pin title, your description and your destination page. Revisit Trends once a month, compare your keywords again, and retire the ones whose lines have gone flat.

8. A monthly keyword routine built on Pinterest Trends. Make Trends part of a short monthly routine. First, check the trend graphs for the keywords your existing pins target — are they still rising, flat, or falling? Second, search five new terms in your niche and compare them, keeping the two strongest. Third, check the seasonal calendar: what peaks in the next 45 days, and what should you publish now to be ready? Fourth, look at the trending topics in your category for one surprise idea. Fifth, turn the best keyword into a pin — title, description, board and destination page all carrying the same term — and note the date so you can measure its impressions two weeks later. For the bigger picture — profile, boards, fresh pins and a weekly routine — read our guide on how to grow on Pinterest in 2026. Trends tells you what people want; the routine makes sure you act on it before your competitors do.

Start today: open Pinterest Trends, type the main keyword of your niche, and look at the shape of the line. Then type two alternatives and compare. In ten minutes you will know which phrase to build your next pin around — and you will have stopped guessing. Keywords are the cheapest advantage on Pinterest, because the data is free and most creators never look at it.
