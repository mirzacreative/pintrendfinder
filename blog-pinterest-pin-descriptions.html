Pinterest Pin Descriptions That Rank in Search

A practical guide to writing Pinterest pin descriptions that get found: keyword placement in the first sentences, natural language instead of stuffing, the right length, hashtags that help, and calls to action — with good and weak examples.

Quick answer: A pin description that ranks puts its main keyword in the first sentence, explains the benefit in plain language in two or three sentences, adds one related phrase naturally, ends with three to five relevant hashtags and one clear call to action. That is the whole formula — and most pins fail it by doing the opposite: a keyword list with no sentence, a clever caption with no keyword at all, or a description so long nobody reads it.

The pin image gets the click, but the description gets the pin found. Pinterest is a search engine, and its search reads your pin title, your description, your board name and your profile — then decides which searches your pin should appear in. A beautiful pin with an empty description can only be found by people who already follow you. The same pin with a well-written description can be found by anyone searching for that topic, for months or years after you publish it. Yet descriptions are the part of pinning most creators rush: a copied caption, a string of hashtags, or nothing at all. This guide shows how to write descriptions that rank, with examples of good and weak ones so you can see the difference immediately.

If you are setting up your account for the first time, start with our Pinterest SEO checklist for beginners — it covers profile, boards and Rich Pins in one page. This guide is about one skill inside that setup: writing the description itself.

1. Put the keyword in the first sentence — and in the title too. Pinterest reads the first words of your description most strongly, and people scanning the feed only see the first line or two before they decide whether to tap. So the main keyword goes first, in a real sentence, not buried at the end. Compare these two openings for the same pin: "Small bedroom ideas: 12 layouts that make a tiny room feel bigger" versus "I loved putting this room together, it was such a fun project!" The first tells Pinterest and the reader exactly what the pin is about; the second tells them nothing a search engine can use. Put the keyword in the pin title as well — title and description together are the strongest ranking signal you control. Use the free PinTrendFinder tool to check which phrases people actually search for before you write, so you are optimising for real demand instead of your own guess.

2. Write natural sentences, not a keyword list. Keyword stuffing — repeating the same phrase five times, or pasting a comma-separated list of keywords where a sentence should be — does not help a pin rank, and it hurts in two ways. First, Pinterest's spam detection recognises stuffed descriptions and can reduce a pin's distribution. Second, real people read descriptions too, and a list of keywords with no sentence in sight does not convince anyone to save or click. Write the way you would describe the pin to a friend: two or three plain sentences, the keyword once in the first sentence, and one related phrase later. "Small bedroom ideas for a rented flat: 12 layouts that need no drilling, no paint and no landlord permission. Save this before your next furniture shop." That reads like a person wrote it, and it still contains the keyword, a related phrase and a reason to save. One keyword, used naturally, beats ten keywords crammed into a list.

3. Get the length right: short enough to read, long enough to rank. Aim for two to four sentences, roughly 100 to 200 characters for the part people actually see, with the most important words in the first sentence. Pinterest allows descriptions up to 500 characters, and you can use the extra space for detail — a second benefit, a note about who the pin is for, or a mention of what the linked page contains. But never put the important information at the end, because most people never expand the description to read it. The practical test: if someone reads only your first sentence, do they know what the pin is and why it matters? If yes, the length is right. If the first sentence is a greeting, a joke or a story with no keyword, rewrite it — the keyword and the benefit belong up front, and the story can come after.

4. Use hashtags sparingly — and only relevant ones. Hashtags on Pinterest work differently from hashtags on Instagram. They make a pin findable in hashtag search, but they carry far less weight than the keywords in your title and description, and Pinterest has been reducing their importance over time. Three to five relevant hashtags at the end of the description are enough — for example, #smallbedroomideas #bedroomdecor #smallspaces. Do not add twenty hashtags, do not repeat your keyword as a hashtag on top of using it in the title and description, and do not use hashtags that have nothing to do with the pin just because they are popular. A long block of hashtags at the end of a description looks like spam to readers, and it signals to Pinterest that the pin was written for the algorithm instead of for people. If you have to choose between a good hashtag and a good sentence, choose the sentence every time.

5. End with one clear call to action. Every description should tell the reader what to do next, in one short sentence. "Save this for your next project." "Click through for the full 12-layout guide." "Try this layout this weekend." A call to action works because Pinterest measures saves and outbound clicks, and those are the signals that push a pin higher in search. A pin that people save tells Pinterest the content is worth showing to more people; a pin that people click tells Pinterest the page behind it is worth visiting. Without a call to action, many people will look at a good pin and scroll past it — they liked it, but nobody asked them to do anything. One call to action is enough. Two or three in the same description cancel each other out, because the reader does not know which one to follow.

6. Good vs weak: three examples you can copy the pattern from. Weak: "Bedroom inspo!!! Love this look so much, what do you think? #bedroom #decor #home #interiordesign #love #instagood #picoftheday". It has no keyword anyone searches for, no benefit, no call to action, and seven generic hashtags. Good: "Small bedroom ideas: 12 layouts that make a tiny room feel bigger, with no drilling and no paint. Save this before your next furniture shop. #smallbedroomideas #bedroomdecor #smallspaces". The keyword is first, the benefit is clear, there is one call to action and three relevant hashtags. Weak: "Recipe in bio, link in bio, click the link!!!" — it says nothing about the dish and gives Pinterest nothing to rank. Good: "Easy 20-minute garlic butter chicken: one pan, five ingredients, dinner on the table fast. Tap through for the full recipe and the shopping list. #easydinner #chickenrecipes #weeknightdinner". Notice the pattern in both good examples: keyword first, benefit second, one call to action, a few relevant hashtags. Copy the pattern, change the words, and your descriptions will rank better than most pins on the platform.

7. Match the description to the board and the page behind the pin. A description does not rank on its own — it ranks together with the pin title, the board it is saved to, and the page it links to. Save the pin to a board whose name contains the same keyword: a pin about small bedroom ideas belongs on a board called "Small Bedroom Ideas", not on a board called "Stuff I Like". For board names that match real search demand, see our guide to Pinterest board name ideas. Then check that the page behind the pin delivers what the description promises: a description that says "12 layouts" must lead to a page with 12 layouts, because a pin that gets clicks but no saves tells Pinterest the content disappointed people, and its distribution drops. Finally, keep your descriptions honest across the account: two different designs for the same page can share a similar description, but copying the identical description onto dozens of unrelated pins looks like spam. Write each description for its own pin, even when the words are similar.

8. A quick description checklist for every pin you publish. Before you hit publish, run through this list: is the main keyword in the first sentence? Does the description say what the reader gets, in plain language? Is there one related phrase, used naturally? Are there three to five relevant hashtags at the end, and no more? Is there one clear call to action? Does the board name match the keyword? Does the linked page deliver what the description promises? If every answer is yes, the description is ready. If any answer is no, fix that one thing before publishing — it takes thirty seconds, and it is the difference between a pin that gets found and a pin that gets ignored. For the bigger picture — profile, boards, fresh pins and a weekly routine — read our guide on how to grow on Pinterest in 2026, and for pin image layout, fonts and colours, see our Pinterest pin design tips.

Start today: pick your five best-performing pins, rewrite their descriptions with the keyword first, one benefit, one call to action and three relevant hashtags, and watch what happens to their impressions over the next two weeks. Then write every new pin description the same way. Descriptions are the cheapest ranking improvement on Pinterest — no new images, no new boards, no extra tools — just better words in the right order.
