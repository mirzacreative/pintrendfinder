Pinterest Rich Pins: Setup Guide 2026 for Creators

A practical guide to Pinterest Rich Pins in 2026: what Rich Pins are, the article, product and recipe types, how to add the metadata to your pages, how to validate and apply, and how to fix the problems that stop the extra details appearing.

Quick answer: A Rich Pin is a pin that shows extra information taken from the page it links to — a headline and author for an article, a price and availability for a product, or ingredients and cooking time for a recipe. To set Rich Pins up, claim your website on a Pinterest business account, add the right metadata to your pages, run a page URL through the Rich Pin validator, and apply. New pins saved after approval should show the extra details. Rich Pins do not guarantee more clicks or a higher ranking, and this guide makes no such claim. Pinterest changes its tools and its help pages often, so if a button or a menu name in this guide looks different on the day you follow it, trust what you see in Pinterest itself and in its current help pages, and check that the page you are validating is the live, public version of the page.

Most pins are simple. You save an image, you write a title and a description, and the pin carries that image, that title and that description wherever it goes. If you change the price on your shop page, the old pin still shows the old price. If you update a blog post headline, the old pin still shows the old headline. A Rich Pin works differently. It reads information from the page itself, and shows that information on the pin, so the pin and the page stay in step. For a blogger, that means the headline and the author travel with the pin. For a seller, it means the price and whether the item is in stock travel with the pin. For a recipe creator, it means the ingredients, the cooking time and the serving size travel with the pin. That extra information helps a person decide, before they click, whether the page is what they want — and a pin that answers the question before the click is a pin that earns the right clicks. This guide explains the setup from the beginning: what Rich Pins are and what they are not, the three main types, what to prepare before you start, how to add the metadata, how to validate and apply, how to test that the setup has worked, and how to fix the common problems. It does not repeat the wider Pinterest growth, description, keyword, board, Idea Pin, Etsy or frequency guides on this site — those are linked where they belong, and each one covers its own topic.

1. What a Rich Pin is, and what it is not. A Rich Pin is a pin with metadata behind it. When someone saves a page from your website, Pinterest reads the metadata on that page — the structured tags in the page code that describe the title, the description, the image, the price, or the recipe details — and displays the relevant pieces on the pin. A standard pin shows the image and the text you typed when you saved it, and that text stays fixed until someone edits the pin. A Rich Pin shows the page's own information, and when the page changes, the pin can show the new information. That is the whole difference, and it is a useful one: a product pin that always shows the current price is more trustworthy than a pin with a price typed in months ago, and an article pin that shows the current headline matches the page the reader lands on.

A Rich Pin is not a different kind of pin that you choose when you upload an image. You do not tick a "Rich Pin" box in the pin creator. The richness comes from the website, not from the upload. If the page has no metadata, the pin saved from it will be a standard pin, however good the image is. If the page has the metadata and the site has been approved, the pin saved from it will be a Rich Pin. That is why the setup in this guide is mostly website work — claiming the site, adding tags, validating — and not pin-creation work. It is also why Rich Pins only work for pins saved from your own website. A pin you upload directly, with no link to a page on your site, has no page to read metadata from, and it will stay a standard pin. And a Rich Pin is not a promise. Pinterest does not guarantee that a Rich Pin will rank higher, get more impressions, or bring more clicks than a standard pin. The extra details make a pin more informative, and informative pins can earn clicks — but the image, the title, the description, the board, and the page itself still do most of the work. Treat Rich Pins as good housekeeping for a website that is already publishing useful pins, not as a shortcut around that work.

2. The three main Rich Pin types. Pinterest has offered several Rich Pin types over the years, and the list of available types can change. The three types this guide covers are the ones most creators and sellers use, and the ones the validator still expects to see: article pins, product pins, and recipe pins. Check Pinterest's current help pages before you start, because a type that was available last year may have changed, and the exact fields Pinterest reads can change too.

Article Rich Pins are for blog posts, news stories, and other written pages. They show the headline, the author, and a short description from the page, alongside the image. They suit bloggers, magazines, and anyone whose main content is an article. If your site is a blog, article pins are the type you will use most, and every new post should carry the metadata so that every pin saved from it shows the right headline. Product Rich Pins are for shop and product pages. They show the price, the currency, and whether the item is in stock, and they can show the product title and description from the page. They suit Etsy sellers, shop owners, and anyone selling a product from their own site. A product pin whose price is out of date is worse than a standard pin with no price, so product metadata must be kept accurate on the page itself — the pin will only ever be as honest as the page it reads. Recipe Rich Pins are for recipe pages. They show the ingredients, the cooking time, and the serving size from the page, so a person can judge the recipe before opening it. They suit food bloggers and recipe sites. A recipe page needs the recipe details in structured form on the page, not only written in the body text, or Pinterest will not be able to read them.

One site can use more than one type. A food blog with a shop can have article pins for its posts, recipe pins for its recipes, and product pins for its shop pages, all at once. The type is decided page by page, by the metadata on that page. A blog post page carries article metadata, a product page carries product metadata, and a recipe page carries recipe metadata. You do not choose one type for the whole site. You add the right metadata to each kind of page, and Pinterest reads what is there. If a page carries no Rich Pin metadata, pins saved from it will be standard pins, and the rest of the site will be unaffected.

3. Before you start: claim the website and prepare the pages. Three things must be true before the metadata work is worth doing. First, your Pinterest account should be a business account, and your website should be claimed on it. Claiming a website means proving to Pinterest that the site is yours, usually by adding a small verification file or a tag to the site, or by adding a record at your domain provider, following the steps Pinterest shows for your account. If the site is not claimed, the validator step later in this guide will not complete for that site. If you are starting a Pinterest account from scratch, read our guide on how to grow on Pinterest in 2026 first, because it covers the account setup and the first-month plan, and Rich Pins sit on top of that foundation rather than replacing it.

Second, your pages must be public and reachable. A page behind a login, a page that only loads after a click, or a page that blocks Pinterest's reader will not validate, however good its metadata is. The page must load over a secure connection, and the metadata must be in the page code that a reader receives when it requests the URL — not added later by a script that only runs in a browser. If you use a website builder or a shop platform, check whether the platform adds the metadata for you. Many platforms add basic Open Graph tags automatically, and some add product or article structured data as well. If your platform already adds the tags, your job may be to check them and fill the gaps, rather than to add everything by hand. Third, prepare one good test page of each type you plan to use: one article page, one product page, or one recipe page, with a real title, a real image, and complete details. You will validate that page first, fix any errors on it, and then roll the same pattern out to the rest of the site. Starting with the whole site at once makes errors hard to find. Starting with one page makes them easy.

4. Add the metadata to your pages. Metadata is information about the page, written in the page code for machines to read. Pinterest reads two kinds of metadata for Rich Pins: Open Graph tags, which describe the page in a standard way, and Schema.org structured data, which describes the page's content in more detail. You do not need to understand the full standards to set this up. You need to know which fields each type expects, and where they go. The tags go in the head of the page — the part of the page code that loads before the visible content — and they must match what the visible page actually says. A product page whose metadata says the price is one figure while the visible page says another will confuse both Pinterest and the buyer, and a recipe page whose metadata lists ingredients that are not on the page will not be trusted.

For an article page, the key fields are the page title, a plain description of the article, the main image, the author name, and the date the article was published. The Open Graph tags carry the title, the description, the image, and the page type, and the structured data carries the article details, including the author and the date. Use the same headline in the metadata as the headline on the page, and the same description as the opening summary, or a short version of it. A headline that says one thing in the metadata and another on the page will look like a mistake to anyone who clicks through. For a product page, the key fields are the product name, the price, the currency, whether the item is in stock, the main image, and a short description. The price and the availability must be kept up to date on the page, because the pin will show them. If a product goes out of stock, the page should say so, and the pin will follow. If the price changes, change it on the page first, and the pin will catch up. Do not put a sale price in the metadata while the page shows the full price, or the other way round. For a recipe page, the key fields are the recipe name, the image, the ingredients list, the cooking time, and the number of servings. The ingredients must be listed as separate items in the structured data, not as one long paragraph, and the cooking time must be given in a form the reader can understand. If you write recipes, keep the recipe details in one place on the page — a recipe card or a structured recipe block — and make sure the metadata and the visible card say the same thing.

If you use a content system, a shop platform, or a plugin, look for its Pinterest, Open Graph, or Schema.org settings before you edit code by hand. Many systems let you fill in the fields in a form, and they write the tags for you. If you edit code by hand, add the tags to your page template — the shared layout used by all pages of that type — rather than to one page at a time, so that every new article, product, or recipe automatically carries the right metadata. After you add the tags, open the live page, view its source, and check that the tags are present and that the values are correct. A tag that is present but empty, or a tag with a placeholder value, is worse than a missing tag, because the validator may pass it and the pin will then show the placeholder. And keep the visible content honest: the title, description, and image in the metadata should match what a reader sees on the page. Good pin descriptions still matter alongside the metadata — our guide to Pinterest pin descriptions that rank explains how to write the description a person reads, which is a different job from the metadata a machine reads, and both need doing.

5. Validate and apply: the Rich Pin validator. Once the metadata is on a live test page, open Pinterest's Rich Pin validator and enter the exact URL of that page. The validator will fetch the page, read the metadata, and report what it found — and what is missing or wrong. Read the report carefully. It will usually name the missing field, or show the value it read, so you can see whether the problem is a missing tag, a tag with the wrong value, or a page the validator could not fetch at all. Fix the errors on the page, save the page, and run the validator again on the same URL. Repeat until the validator reports that the page is ready, and then use the validator's apply step for your site. The apply step tells Pinterest that your claimed site is ready for Rich Pins, and it applies to the site, not to a single page — so once it is done, new pins saved from other pages on the same site, with the right metadata, should show as Rich Pins without you validating every page one by one. That said, it is still worth validating one page of each type, because an article page and a product page use different fields, and a site can be approved for one type while another type still has errors.

Save a fresh test pin after approval, from the test page itself, using the save button on the page or by saving the page URL in Pinterest. Check the pin: does it show the extra details — the headline and author, the price and availability, or the ingredients and time? If it does, the setup has worked for that type. If it shows only the image and the text you typed, the setup has not worked yet, and the troubleshooting section below is the next step. Do not judge the result from an old pin saved before approval. Old pins may keep their old appearance, and only new pins saved after approval reliably show the new details. Save a new pin, from the live page, and judge from that one.

6. Troubleshooting: why the details do not appear. Work through the checks in order, and change one thing at a time, or you will not know which change fixed the problem. First, confirm the website is claimed on the same business account you are using, and that the claim is still valid. A claim that has lapsed, or a site claimed on a different account, is a common cause of a validator that will not apply. Second, open the live page in a browser and confirm the metadata is present in the page source, with the right values. A page that looks right in your editor but has not been published, or a page served from a cache that still shows the old version, will fail validation for a reason that has nothing to do with the tags. Third, run the validator on the exact URL, including any trailing characters, and read the full error report. A validator error that names a missing field is a gift: add that field, republish, and validate again. Fourth, check that the page is not blocking Pinterest's reader. A page that requires a login, a page that blocks automated readers, or a page whose server returns an error to the validator will not validate, even with perfect metadata. Fifth, save a brand-new pin from the page after the fix. Re-saving an old pin, or editing an old pin's title, will not always refresh the Rich Pin details. And sixth, allow time. The extra details do not always appear instantly on every pin, and checking the same pin five minutes after approval can give a misleading result. If the details still do not appear after a few days, and the validator reports no errors, check Pinterest's current help pages for a change in the feature, and check whether the page type you are using is still supported.

Two other problems are worth naming. The first is mixed content: a page served over a secure connection that loads its main image over an insecure connection can fail to show the image properly, and the pin may fall back to a standard pin. Serve the page and its images over a secure connection. The second is a template problem: a tag added to one page but not to the template will be missing from the next page you publish, and the problem will return page by page. Fix the template, not the page, and validate a newly published page to prove the template is right. If you sell on Etsy as well as on your own site, remember that Rich Pins apply to pins saved from your own claimed website, and the Etsy-specific workflow is different — our guide to Pinterest SEO for Etsy sellers covers how Etsy listings and Pinterest work together, and it should be read alongside this guide rather than instead of it.

7. Using Rich Pins well, once they are set up. Setup is the beginning, not the end. A Rich Pin still needs a good image, a keyword title, and a plain description, and it still needs to be saved to the board whose topic matches it. The metadata supplies the extra details; it does not write the pin for you. Use the free PinTrendFinder tool to check the keywords for each pin, and our guide on how to use Pinterest Trends to find keywords to see which phrases hold steady interest and which are seasonal. Save each Rich Pin to the board that matches its topic, not to every board at once — our guide to Pinterest board SEO explains how board titles, descriptions, and pin placement work together, and a Rich Pin on the wrong board is a good pin in the wrong place.

Keep the posting rhythm steady. Rich Pins do not change how often you should post: a realistic starting point is still a few fresh pins a week, kept up for months, as explained in our guide to how often to post on Pinterest in 2026. A Rich Pin is a fresh pin when the image or the idea is new, even if the page is old — a new image for an existing product or article, saved after approval, will show the Rich Pin details. And Rich Pins sit alongside the other formats, not instead of them. Idea Pins, which carry no outbound link, are good at showing a process and turning a viewer into a follower, and our guide to Pinterest Idea Pins in 2026 explains where they fit. A week with standard and Rich Pins that link to your pages, plus the occasional Idea Pin that shows the making of a product, is a balanced week. A week with only Idea Pins is a week with an audience and no traffic, because Idea Pins do not link out.

Finally, keep the pages honest. Check the product pages whose prices change, the article pages whose headlines change, and the recipe pages whose times change, and make sure the metadata changes with them. A Rich Pin is a promise that the pin and the page agree. If they stop agreeing, the pin will mislead the person who clicks, and that person will not click the next one. A monthly check — pick five pages, save fresh test pins, and confirm the details shown are the details on the pages — takes a few minutes, and it keeps the setup working. If you are building the wider Pinterest workflow from scratch, our guide on how to grow on Pinterest in 2026 gives the account setup and the first-month plan that this setup builds on, and the keyword, description, and board guides linked above fill in the rest.

Start with one page. Claim the site, add the metadata to one article, product, or recipe page, validate that page, apply, and save a fresh test pin. When the extra details appear, roll the same template out to the rest of the site, validate one page of each type, and then keep the metadata in step with the pages as they change. The setup takes an afternoon. The benefit lasts for every pin saved from your site from then on — a pin that shows the price, the headline, or the cooking time before the click, and a page that matches the pin when the click comes. That is all a Rich Pin promises, and it is enough.
