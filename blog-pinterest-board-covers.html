Pinterest Board Covers: Design Covers That Get Clicks

A practical guide to Pinterest board covers: what a cover is, why it matters, how to design one that states the topic plainly and looks clear at a small size, and how to build a consistent set of covers for a whole profile — without guessing at numbers, and without promising results no guide can promise.

Quick answer: A board cover is the image that represents a board on your profile. Choose or design one that names the board's topic in a few plain words, shows one clear image of the result or the idea, uses consistent colours and type across your boards, and stays legible when it is shown small on a phone. Keep the important content in the centre, with margins around it, check the cover on a phone and on a computer after you save it, and make sure the pins inside the board match the promise the cover makes. A cover cannot fix a board with a vague title, an empty description, or pins that do not belong together. Fix the board first, using our Pinterest board SEO guide, then design the cover. This guide assumes the board setup is already sound, and it covers the visual side only: the image, the words, the layout, the branding, and the workflow for building a set of covers that work together.

A visitor decides whether to open a board in a second, and the cover is most of that decision. On a profile page, a visitor sees a grid of boards, each reduced to a title, a small image, and a count of pins. The title is read, but the image is felt first: a clear, bright, specific image invites a tap, while a dark, cluttered, or generic image is passed over, however good the pins inside may be. That is not a judgement on the quality of the work inside the board. It is a judgement on the signal the cover sends, and the signal can be improved without changing a single pin. A good cover does three jobs at once. It tells the visitor what the board is about, in words and in picture, without needing the visitor to read the description. It tells the visitor that the board is cared for, because a designed cover signals a curated collection rather than a dumping ground. And it tells the visitor what to expect inside, so that the visitor who opens the board finds the pins the cover promised, and stays. A cover that does only one of those jobs — a pretty image with no topic, a topic with no image, or a promise the pins do not keep — is a cover that wastes its one second.

1. What a board cover is, and where visitors see it. A board cover is the representative image for a board. If you do not choose one, Pinterest usually shows an automatic collage made from the first pins on the board, which changes as pins are added and which may show a pin that does not represent the board well. If you do choose one, Pinterest shows the image you chose, or the pin you chose, in the board's place on your profile, in board search results, and in other views where the board is shown as a unit. The cover is therefore the board's shop window, and it is seen more often than any single pin on the board, because every visit to your profile shows every board's cover at once. That repetition is why consistency matters: a profile whose covers share a style looks like a planned collection, while a profile whose covers clash looks like a collection of accidents, even when the pins inside are equally good. The cover is not a pin, and it should not be designed as if it were. A pin carries a link, a title, and a description, and it is judged on whether it earns a click to a page. A cover carries no link of its own. It is judged on whether it earns an opening of the board, and the opening is earned by clarity, not by cleverness.

The cover also appears in places you may not think to check. A board shared by link, a board shown in a search result, and a board previewed on a phone may each crop or size the cover differently, and a cover designed only for the profile grid may look wrong in one of those views. Check the cover in each place you can: your profile on a phone, your profile on a computer, the board itself, and a shared link opened in a browser. The check takes a minute, and it catches the two most common cover failures: text cut off at the edges, and an image that is clear in the editor and unreadable at the size Pinterest actually shows. A cover that passes all four views is a cover you can trust. A cover that passes only the editor view is a cover you have not really seen. Keep a note of where you checked, and re-check after Pinterest changes its layout, because a layout change can change the crop without warning, and a cover that was perfect last year may be cropped wrong this year.

2. The board first: a cover cannot rescue a weak board. Before you design a cover, confirm the board itself is sound, because a cover on a weak board is a polished sign on an empty shop. The board title should be a keyword phrase people search for, in the words of the audience, not a personal label only you understand. Our guide to board SEO explains the title, description, and category setup in detail, and our post on board name ideas is a useful companion when the title itself needs work. The board description should state, in a sentence or two, what the board contains and who it is for, in the same plain words as the title. The board should contain enough pins to look like a collection — a board with three pins looks abandoned, however good the cover — and the pins should belong together, on the topic the title promises. Pins that do not belong should be moved or removed before the cover is designed, because the cover will be judged against the pins inside, and a mismatch will disappoint the visitor who opens the board.

Order matters inside the board as well as on the profile. The first pins a visitor sees, after opening the board, set the expectation for the rest, so place the strongest, most representative pins first, and keep the board filled steadily, as our guide to how often to post on Pinterest explains for the publishing rhythm. A board that is fed regularly looks alive, and a board that has not been touched for months looks finished, even if the topic is evergreen. The cover is the last step in the board setup, not the first. Title, description, pins, order, and then cover, in that order, and the cover will have a board worth representing. If you are starting a new board, publish a first batch of pins, let the board take shape, and then design the cover from the strongest pin or image on the board, rather than designing a cover for a board that does not yet exist. The cover should grow out of the board, not be imposed on it.

3. What a good cover shows: one idea, one image, a few words. A good board cover shows one idea, and it shows it plainly. The visitor should understand the board's topic in a glance, without reading a paragraph, and the glance should be satisfied by a single clear image and a few words. The image should be the finished result, the hero object, or the scene the board is about: a finished room for a home decor board, a finished dish for a recipe board, a finished outfit for a style board, a finished craft for a craft board. The image should be bright, sharp, and uncluttered, with the subject large in the frame and the background simple, because a small image with a busy background becomes noise at profile size. Avoid collages inside a cover, unless the collage is the point of the board, because several small images inside one small cover become several unreadable images. One image, large, is almost always clearer than four images, small. If the board's topic is a process rather than a result — a tutorial board, a how-to board — show the step that looks most like the result, or the tools laid out plainly, rather than a mid-process image that needs explaining.

The words on a cover should name the topic, not sell it. A cover that says "Small Bedroom Storage Ideas" names the topic. A cover that says "You Won't Believe These Amazing Ideas!!!" names nothing, and it reads as noise. Use the board's keyword phrase, or a short form of it, in the same words as the board title, so that the cover and the title reinforce each other rather than competing. Keep the wording to a handful of words — a short phrase, not a sentence — because a cover is read at a glance, and a sentence will not be read at all at profile size. If the board serves a specific audience, name it in the phrase where it fits naturally: "Small Bedroom Storage" is clearer than "Storage", and "Beginner Sourdough Recipes" is clearer than "Recipes", because the qualifier tells the visitor whether the board is for them. Do not add a web address, a username, or a call to action to a cover, unless the cover is also used elsewhere where that information is needed. A cover's job is to name the topic and invite an opening. The profile, the pins, and the linked pages do the rest.

4. Size, shape, and the safe centre: designing for the crop. Pinterest does not show a board cover at one fixed size in one fixed shape. It shows it in a grid, in a search result, and in a board view, each with its own size and crop, and the shape can change when Pinterest updates its layout. The safe way to design is therefore to design large, and to keep the important content in the centre. Start with a large, high-resolution design, so that the cover stays sharp when it is shown large, and so that a crop does not leave it soft. Keep the title text, the main subject, and any branding well inside the centre of the design, with generous margins on all sides, so that a crop at the edges, in any view, does not cut off a word or a face. Do not place text, a logo, or a key detail at the very edge of the design, where the first crop will remove it. Do not rely on the exact shape shown in your design tool, because the tool's preview is not Pinterest's display. Test the design on your own profile, on a phone and on a computer, and adjust the margins until the cover reads clearly in every view.

The shape question deserves a direct answer, because it is the question beginners ask first. There is no single safe shape to quote as a rule, because Pinterest's display shape changes, and a number quoted today may be wrong after the next layout update. The durable rule is the safe centre: design with the important content in the middle third, keep the edges clear, and test the result where visitors will see it. If you are reusing a pin image as a cover, remember that pins are usually tall, while a board cover is shown wider and shorter, so a tall pin image will be cropped heavily when used as a cover. A tall image with the subject in the middle will survive that crop. A tall image with the subject at the top or the bottom will not. If you are designing a cover from scratch, design it in a wide, landscape shape, with the subject and the text centred, and test it before you commit to it for a whole set of boards. A cover designed for the crop, and tested in the crop, will outlast any number quoted in a guide, including this one.

5. Text on a cover: legibility at a glance, on a phone. Text on a board cover must be legible at the size the cover is actually seen, which is small, on a phone, in a grid, among other boards. That is a stricter test than legibility in a design tool, where the cover is shown large and alone. Choose a typeface that is plain, bold, and easy to read at a small size, and use it at a size that leaves the words large in the frame. A good test is to shrink the design to the size of a postage stamp, or to hold your phone at arm's length, and to ask whether the words can still be read without effort. If they cannot, the type is too small, the words are too many, or the contrast is too weak. Use a short phrase, as above, and give the words room: a phrase that fills the whole cover, edge to edge, will feel crowded and will be harder to read than the same phrase with space around it. Use one typeface for the cover text across all your boards, in one or two weights, so that the set of covers reads as a family, and reserve a second typeface, if you use one, for a small supporting line, such as a qualifier or a count, rather than for the main phrase.

Contrast is the other half of legibility. Dark text on a light background, or light text on a dark background, with a clear difference between the two, is the reliable choice. Text placed directly over a busy photograph, without a backing shape, is the unreliable choice, because the photograph's light and dark areas will fight the text, and some words will vanish into the image. If the text must sit over a photograph, place a solid or semi-solid backing shape behind it, darken or lighten the area behind the text, or place the text in a clear area of the image, such as the sky or a plain wall. Check the contrast on a phone, in daylight, not only on a computer screen in a dark room, because a cover that reads on a monitor may fail on a phone in bright light. Avoid decorative typefaces, script typefaces, and all-caps phrases for the main cover text, because they are harder to read at a small size, and avoid punctuation that adds noise, such as multiple exclamation marks. A cover's text is a label, not an advertisement. It should read like a label: plain, clear, and quick.

6. Colour, branding, and consistency across the profile. A set of board covers should look like a set, and the set is built from a small number of repeated choices: two or three colours, one typeface, one layout pattern, and one kind of image. Choose colours that suit the account's topic and that stay distinct from each other, so that neighbouring boards in the grid can be told apart at a glance. A profile whose every cover uses the same colour, with no variation, will look flat, and a profile whose every cover uses a different colour, with no pattern, will look scattered. The middle course is a shared palette with variation: the same background colour family, with a different accent colour or a different hero image for each board, or the same layout, with a different photograph for each topic. Use the account's existing colours, if it has them, from the profile, the website, or the shop, so that the Pinterest profile, the website, and the shop read as one brand. If the account has no colours yet, choose a palette that suits the topic — calm and light for a home or wellness account, bright and warm for a food or craft account — and use it consistently, rather than choosing a new colour for each board.

Branding on a cover should be light, not loud. A small logo, a small username, or a consistent colour band is enough to mark the cover as yours, and it should sit in the same place on every cover, so that a visitor learns where to look for it. A large logo, a web address, or a watermark across the middle of the cover competes with the topic, and it makes the cover look like an advertisement rather than a collection. If the boards are for a shop, a small shop name in the same place on each cover is a reasonable choice, because it tells the visitor who curates the collection. If the boards are for a blog, a small blog name serves the same purpose. If the boards are personal, no branding is needed at all, and consistency of colour and type will do the work. The branding should survive the crop, so place it in the safe centre, not at the edge, and keep it small enough that it does not distract from the topic phrase. A visitor who opens the board because the topic was clear will find the profile and the shop from the board itself. A visitor who is put off by a loud cover will not open the board at all.

7. Cover styles by board type: topics, seasons, shops, and personal boards. Different kinds of boards benefit from different cover styles, and a single style applied to every board will fit some boards badly. A topic board — a board about one subject, such as small bedrooms, sourdough, or wedding flowers — benefits from a hero image of the finished result, with the topic phrase in plain type. The image is the proof that the board contains the result, and the phrase is the label. A tutorial or how-to board benefits from an image that shows the making or the steps, with a phrase that names the skill, such as "Beginner Knitting Tutorials", because the visitor is looking for a process, not a product. A shop board, for an Etsy shop or a product range, benefits from a clean product image on a plain background, with the product type named plainly, because the visitor is comparing products, and a cluttered cover will lose the comparison. Our guide to Pinterest SEO for Etsy sellers covers the shop workflow, and the cover is the shop window for that workflow.

Seasonal boards need a different rhythm. A board for a holiday or a season — Christmas, Halloween, summer, back to school — should have a cover that names the season plainly and shows the seasonal image, and it should be updated or promoted as the season approaches, then left in place, with the cover still accurate, after the season ends. A seasonal cover left on a board that has moved on to the next season will mislead, so review seasonal covers at the start of each season, and either update the cover or move the board down the profile order. A personal board — a board for your own inspiration, a wish list, or a private collection — needs no custom cover at all, unless it is also a public board that visitors will judge the profile by. A public profile is judged by its public boards, and the boards at the top of the profile matter most, because they are seen first. Spend the cover effort there: the main topic boards, the shop boards, and the boards you link to from your website or your other profiles. The boards at the bottom of the profile, the personal boards, and the boards that are rarely visited can keep the automatic collage, without harming the profile.

8. The workflow: from board audit to a finished set of covers. Build covers in a batch, not one at a time, because a batch keeps the style consistent and the work efficient. Start with an audit: list your boards, in profile order, note each board's title, description, pin count, and whether it has a custom cover, and mark the boards that matter most. Our Pinterest SEO checklist is a useful audit tool for the board setup, and the cover audit can sit alongside it in the same document. Next, choose the style: the colours, the typeface, the layout pattern, and the kind of image, and make one test cover for one board, in the full workflow, from design to profile check. Do not make the whole set before testing one cover, because a style that looks good in the design tool may fail on a phone, and it is cheaper to fix one cover than twenty. Test the cover on a phone and on a computer, in the profile grid, in the board view, and in a shared link, and adjust the margins, the type size, and the contrast until it reads clearly in every view.

Once the test cover passes, make the set, board by board, in profile order, using the same template, with the same colours, typeface, and layout, and a different hero image and phrase for each board. Keep the source files, with the board name, the date, and the image source noted, in one folder, so that a cover can be remade or updated without starting from scratch. Save the finished covers in a consistent format, at a large size, and apply them one board at a time, checking each board on your profile after you save it, rather than applying all of them and checking at the end. A batch of ten covers will take an afternoon, and it will repay the time every time a visitor opens your profile. Keep a record of the set — the board, the cover file, the date applied, and the hero image source — in the same document as the board audit, so that the next update, whether for a season, a new board, or a style change, starts from a record rather than from a guess. And keep the covers honest: a cover that shows an image from a pin on the board is honest, because the visitor will find that image inside. A cover that shows a stock image of a topic the board does not contain is a promise the board cannot keep.

9. Tools, templates, and images: what to use, and what to avoid. Any design tool that lets you place an image, add text, and export a large image will serve for board covers, and the choice of tool matters less than the discipline of the template. A template is a saved design with the colours, typeface, layout, and margins already set, so that each new cover starts from the same place and only the image and the phrase change. Make the template once, test it, and reuse it, rather than designing each cover from a blank canvas, because a blank canvas invites variation, and variation breaks the set. If you use a template from a library, adapt it to your colours, your typeface, and your layout, and remove any element that does not serve the topic, rather than using it as it comes. A template that is used unchanged, by many accounts, will look generic, and a generic cover will not distinguish your boards from any other account's. The template is a starting point. The colours, the type, and the image make it yours.

Images for covers should be your own, or images you have the right to use, and they should be sharp, bright, and relevant to the board. A photograph you took, a product photograph from your shop, or an image from a pin already on the board are the safest sources, because they are yours, or they are already part of the board, and they will be found inside. A stock image is acceptable if you have the right to use it, and if it honestly represents the board, but a stock image that is used by many accounts will not distinguish your board, and a stock image of a different topic will mislead. Avoid images with text already baked into them, unless the text is the topic phrase and it is legible, because baked-in text will compete with the cover text you add. Avoid images with a watermark, a logo, or a credit line from another source, because they will look borrowed. And avoid images that are dark, blurry, or low-resolution, because a cover is judged at a glance, and a poor image will be judged as a poor board, however good the pins inside. For the pin images that sit inside the board, our guide to pin design tips and our guide to pin descriptions cover the image and text choices in detail, and the cover and the pins should share the same clarity, even though they do different jobs.

10. Testing a cover: the phone test, the grid test, and the mismatch test. Test every cover before you trust it, with three checks that take a few minutes together. The phone test: open your profile on a phone, scroll to the board, and ask whether the topic can be read, without zooming, at the size shown. If the words cannot be read, or the image cannot be understood, the cover fails, however good it looks in the design tool. The grid test: look at the profile as a whole, with all the boards visible, and ask whether the set reads as a set, and whether each board can be told apart from its neighbours. If two neighbouring covers look alike, change the image or the accent colour on one of them, so that the grid is varied as well as consistent. The mismatch test: open the board, look at the first pins, and ask whether the cover's promise is kept. If the cover shows a finished room and the board opens with unrelated pins, the cover has misled the visitor, and the board, not the cover, needs fixing. A cover that passes all three tests is a cover worth keeping. A cover that fails one test should be fixed before the next board is covered, because the same failure will repeat across the set if the template is wrong.

Test with a fresh eye where you can. Show the profile to someone who does not know the account, and ask them to name the topic of each board from the cover alone, without reading the title. If they can name it, the cover works. If they hesitate, or name a different topic, the cover needs a clearer image or a plainer phrase. Test again after a month, with the analytics check described in our Pinterest analytics guide, by looking at which boards earn profile visits, saves, and outbound clicks, and whether the boards with new covers behave differently from the boards without them. Do not treat a single week's numbers as a verdict on a cover, because analytics moves for many reasons, and a cover is one variable among many. Look for a pattern over a month, across several boards, before changing a cover that passes the three tests. And keep the test record with the cover record, so that the next set of covers starts from evidence, not from a feeling.

11. Updating covers: seasons, new boards, and style changes. Covers are not set-and-forget. A cover should be reviewed when the board changes, when the season changes, when the account's style changes, and when Pinterest changes its layout. A board that has grown from a narrow topic to a wider one needs a cover that names the wider topic, or a split into two boards, each with its own cover. A board that has been renamed needs a cover with the new phrase, because a cover with the old phrase will contradict the new title. A seasonal board needs a cover review at the start of each season, as above. A style change — new colours, a new typeface, a new layout — should be applied to the whole set, in a batch, rather than to one board at a time, because a half-changed set will look inconsistent for months. And a Pinterest layout change should trigger a check of every cover, on a phone and on a computer, because a crop change can cut off text that was previously safe.

When you update a cover, keep the old cover file, with the date it was replaced, in the cover folder, so that you can revert if the new cover performs worse or looks wrong. Note the reason for the update — a new title, a new season, a layout change, a test result — in the cover record, so that the history of the board is visible. Do not update covers too often, because a cover that changes every week will confuse returning visitors, who learn to find a board by its cover, and will make the analytics record hard to read, because the cover will be a moving variable. A cover should be stable for a season, or for a few months, and changed for a reason, not for novelty. The exception is a cover that fails the three tests, or that misleads visitors, which should be fixed at once, however recently it was made. A misleading cover is not a style choice. It is a broken promise, and it should be fixed before the next visitor sees it.

12. Mistakes that waste the cover's one second. The most common cover mistakes are all versions of the same error: asking a small image to do too much. Too many words, too small a typeface, too busy an image, too many images in a collage, too loud a logo, and too clever a phrase all waste the second the visitor gives the cover. The second mistake is inconsistency: a set of covers with no shared style, no shared colours, and no shared typeface, which reads as a collection of accidents. The third is mismatch: a cover that promises a topic the pins do not deliver, which disappoints the visitor who opens the board and which teaches the visitor not to trust the next cover. The fourth is the edge: text, a logo, or a face placed at the edge of the design, where the crop removes it, leaving a cover that looks broken. The fifth is the borrowed image: a stock image, a watermarked image, or an image from another account, which looks generic or borrowed, and which may not be yours to use. The sixth is the seasonal cover left in place out of season, which signals a neglected profile. And the seventh is the cover designed only in the editor, never checked on a phone, which is a cover that has never been seen as a visitor sees it. Each mistake is easy to fix, and each fix is the same: one idea, one image, a few plain words, a consistent style, a promise the board keeps, a safe centre, an image you have the right to use, a seasonal review, and a phone check before you publish.

13. Accessibility and honesty: covers for every visitor, and no false promise. A cover should be accessible to every visitor, including visitors who use a screen reader, who see colours differently, or who view the profile on a small, dim screen. The board title and description, which screen readers read, should state the topic plainly, in the same words as the cover, so that the cover's meaning is available without the image. The text on the cover should have strong contrast with its background, as above, and should not rely on colour alone to convey meaning, because colour alone will not survive a greyscale view or a colour-vision difference. The image should be relevant and honest, not a stock image of a different topic, and the cover should not claim a result the board does not contain, such as a product, a price, or a guarantee. A cover is a label, and a label's first duty is honesty. A visitor who opens a board and finds what the cover promised will stay, save, and click. A visitor who opens a board and finds something else will leave, and will be slower to trust the next cover. That trust, built board by board, is the real value of a good set of covers, and it is worth more than any single click.

Finally, keep the cover in its place. A cover is one part of a board, and a board is one part of a profile, and the profile is one part of a Pinterest presence that also includes pins, descriptions, trends, and analytics. The cover will not, on its own, bring traffic, sales, or followers, and no guide should promise that it will. What a good cover does, reliably, is make a good board easier to find, easier to understand, and easier to open, and that is a worthwhile job, done well, board by board. Start with the board that matters most, design one cover, test it on a phone, and build the set from there. Frequently asked questions

What is a Pinterest board cover?

A Pinterest board cover is the image shown for a board on your profile and in search and feed views of the board, instead of the automatic collage Pinterest makes from the pins on the board. You choose it from a pin already saved to the board, or by uploading a cover image where Pinterest offers that option, and it is the first visual impression a visitor gets of what the board contains. A good cover states the topic plainly, matches the pins inside, and looks clear at a small size on a phone. A cover that misleads, or that shows a different topic from the pins inside, will disappoint the visitor who opens the board.

How do I change a Pinterest board cover?

Open the board, open its edit or settings view, and look for the cover option, which Pinterest usually places near the board title and description. From there you can usually choose a pin already on the board as the cover, or upload a new cover image, then save the board. The exact labels and layout change from time to time as Pinterest updates its tools, and the option may appear in a different place on a phone and on a computer, so look for the cover setting in the board's edit view rather than searching for a button name from an old guide. After saving, open your profile on a phone and on a computer to check how the cover actually looks.

Should every board have a custom cover?

Custom covers are most worthwhile for the boards that matter most: the boards that carry your main topics, that appear on your profile first, or that you link to from elsewhere. A focused account with a few strong boards, each with a clear custom cover, will look more organised than an account with dozens of boards and no covers at all. Boards that are personal, seasonal, or rarely visited can keep the automatic collage, and a cover is not a substitute for a good board title, description, and pins. Fix the board setup first, using our board SEO guide, then add covers to the boards that earn the most attention.

What size should a Pinterest board cover be?

Design the cover at a large, high-resolution size and check how Pinterest crops and displays it on a phone and on a computer before you publish it, because the displayed shape and crop can change when Pinterest updates its layout. Keep the important content — the title text, the main image, and any branding — well inside the centre of the design, with generous margins, so that a crop at the edges does not cut it off. Use a sharp, clear source image, legible text at the size the cover will actually be seen, and a design that still reads when it is shown small. Test the cover on your own profile before you judge it, and re-check it after any Pinterest layout change.
