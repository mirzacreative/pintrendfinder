Pinterest Analytics: How to Read Your Stats & Act

A practical guide to Pinterest analytics: what each number means, which patterns are worth acting on, which ones are noise, and how to turn a weekly check into better pins, better boards, and better pages — without guessing, and without promising results no guide can promise.

Quick answer: Start with a business account and a claimed website, then check your analytics once a week. Read impressions as how often pins were shown, saves as how often pins were kept, pin clicks as how often pins were opened, and outbound clicks as how often people left Pinterest for your page. Compare like with like — the same period length, the same kind of pin — look for patterns across several pins, and change one thing at a time: a title style, an image style, a board, or a topic. Analytics does not tell you what to publish next. It tells you what your last pins did, and a careful reader can turn that record into a better next batch. This guide explains how to read that record. It assumes the setup is already done; if it is not, read our Pinterest SEO starter guide for beginners first, and use our Pinterest SEO checklist to confirm the profile, boards, and pins are in place before you judge any numbers.

Pinterest analytics frightens beginners for the wrong reason. It is not the numbers that are hard. It is the habit of opening the dashboard, seeing a line go up or down, and deciding, on the spot, that something has worked or failed. A single day's line is mostly noise. A single pin's first week is mostly a guess. A month of steady publishing, read as a pattern, is evidence. The difference between a creator who improves and a creator who churns is rarely talent or budget. It is the discipline of reading the record slowly, writing down what was published and when, and changing one variable at a time. This guide teaches that discipline in the order you will use it: where the numbers live, what each metric means, how to read the overview, the pin view, the board view, and the audience view, how to connect analytics with search and trends, how to turn a pattern into a publishing decision, and how to avoid the misreadings that waste the most time. No figure in this guide is presented as a benchmark, and no outcome is promised, because the right numbers for a recipe account, a home decor account, and an Etsy shop are different, and a guide that invents a universal target would be inventing it. What follows is a method for finding your own numbers, and for acting on them honestly.

1. Get to the numbers: business account, claimed website, and a clean start. Pinterest analytics is available on a business account, not as a full feature on a personal account. If you have a personal account, convert it to a business account in the settings; your pins and boards carry over, and nothing you have published is lost. If you are starting fresh, create a business account directly. Next, claim your website, following the steps Pinterest shows for your account — usually a verification tag or file on the site, or a record at your domain provider. Claiming links your pins to your site, lets Pinterest attribute traffic from pins saved from your site, and is a requirement for some features, including Rich Pins. If your website is not claimed, your analytics will still show your pins, but the picture of traffic to your site will be incomplete, and any conclusion you draw about clicks will be weaker than it should be. Do the claiming step before you start judging numbers, and do it once, properly. From your profile, analytics is reached from the top menu, where Pinterest groups it into an overview, a view of your pins, and a view of your audience. The exact layout changes from time to time as Pinterest updates its tools, so this guide names the views by what they show rather than by the exact label on a button that may have moved. If a view has moved, look for the same idea — profile overview, pin performance, audience — under the analytics menu, and you will find it.

Before the first serious check, do three housekeeping jobs. First, confirm the date range. Analytics always shows a range — a day, a week, a month, a custom range — and the range decides what the numbers mean. A pin published yesterday will look quiet in a 30-day view and loud in a 7-day view, and neither view is wrong. Second, note what you published, and when. Keep a simple record — a document or a spreadsheet — with the pin title, the image style, the board it went to, the page it links to, and the date it was published. Without that record, analytics is a set of numbers with no causes attached, and you will not be able to tell whether a good week was caused by a good pin, a good board, a seasonal topic, or luck. Third, give new pins time. Pinterest needs time to understand a new pin and to show it to the right people, and a pin can keep gathering saves and clicks long after it was published. A pin judged after two days has barely been seen. A pin judged after a month has a record worth reading. The weekly check in this guide is a habit, not a verdict: each week you note what happened, and each month you decide what to change.

2. The metrics, in plain language — and what each one cannot tell you. Every metric in Pinterest analytics answers one question, and no metric answers two. Impressions count how often a pin was shown on screen — in search results, in the home feed, or on a board. Impressions measure distribution: how often Pinterest put the pin in front of people. They do not measure interest, quality, or clicks. A pin with many impressions and few clicks was shown widely and acted on rarely, which is information about the pin's appeal or its match to the audience, not a failure of distribution. Engagements count the interactions a pin received — saves, clicks, and other actions Pinterest groups under engagement. Engagement is a summary, not a single behaviour, so always look at the parts that make it up before you act on it. Saves count how often a person kept a pin to one of their own boards. A save is a vote for later: the person wants the idea again, or wants to show it to someone else. Saves are the closest Pinterest has to a long-term signal, because a saved pin can be found again, and can be shown to the saver's followers. Pin clicks count how often a person clicked a pin to open it and see it close up, read the description, and see the link. A pin click is a vote for more detail: the image and title were interesting enough to open. Outbound clicks count how often a person clicked through from a pin to the linked website or shop. An outbound click is the step that brings traffic, and it is the metric that matters most if your goal is visits, sales, or sign-ups. Profile visits count how often a person opened your profile, and follows count how often a person followed your account. Both are signs of interest in the account as a whole, rather than in one pin, and both move slowly.

Each metric has a limit, and the limit is where beginners go wrong. Impressions cannot tell you whether the right people saw the pin. Saves cannot tell you whether the person ever used the idea. Pin clicks cannot tell you whether the person liked what they found. Outbound clicks cannot tell you what the person did on your site — whether they stayed, read, bought, or left at once. For that last question, you need your own site's analytics, read alongside Pinterest's, and the two will rarely match exactly, because they count different things in different ways. Do not chase a single metric. A pin that earns saves but no outbound clicks may be a beautiful idea with a weak link or a weak reason to click. A pin that earns outbound clicks but no saves may be a useful page with an image nobody wants to keep. A pin that earns impressions but nothing else may be shown to the wrong audience, or may be clear enough that the image alone satisfies the searcher. Read the metrics together, for the same pin, over the same period, and ask what story the combination tells. That story — not any one number — is the unit of analysis in this guide.

3. Reading the overview: trends, comparisons, and the noise in between. The overview is the profile-level view: how the account as a whole performed over the chosen period, shown as totals and as a line over time. Start with the totals for the period, then look at the line. The line matters more than the totals, because the line shows the shape: steady, rising, falling, spiky, or flat. A steady line with small ups and downs is a healthy account publishing steadily. A rising line that climbs for weeks is a pattern worth understanding — what changed? A falling line that falls for weeks is a pattern worth investigating — what stopped? A spiky line, with one high day and many low days, usually means one pin did well for a day, or one day was seasonal, and it tells you little about the account. A flat line at a low level usually means the account is publishing too little, or the pins are not being shown, and the fix is in the setup — the checklist — rather than in the numbers. Compare the period with the previous period of the same length: this 28 days with the previous 28 days, or this week with last week. Pinterest's comparison view does that arithmetic for you. Read the comparison as a direction — up, down, or flat — and as a size — a little, or a lot — and then ask what you published, or stopped publishing, in the period that changed. A comparison without a publishing record is a direction without a cause.

Watch for three traps in the overview. The first is the new-account trap: a new account's numbers rise fast from a very low base, and the rise looks dramatic because the base was near zero. It is not dramatic. It is the account being found for the first time. Judge a new account by whether the setup is complete and the publishing habit is steady, not by the steepness of the first line. The second is the seasonal trap: a topic that rises before a holiday, a season, or an event will fall afterwards, and the fall is not a failure. It is the season ending. Our guide on how to use Pinterest Trends to find keywords explains how to tell a seasonal rise from a lasting one, and it is worth reading before you react to a seasonal fall. The third is the single-day trap: one day's spike, caused by one pin being shown widely for a day, or by a search surge, will distort a weekly average. Look at the line, not the average, and look at the week, not the day. If the line returns to its usual level the next day, the spike was an event, not a change. Note it, and move on. The overview's job is to tell you the account's direction. The pin view's job, next, is to tell you which pins caused it.

4. Reading your top pins: sorting, grouping, and asking the right question. The pin view lists your pins, with their metrics for the chosen period, and lets you sort by each metric. Sort by impressions first, and note the pins Pinterest showed most. Then sort by saves, and note the pins people kept. Then sort by outbound clicks, and note the pins that sent traffic. The three lists will overlap, but they will not be the same list, and the differences are the interesting part. A pin high on impressions and low on saves and clicks is a pin that is shown but not chosen: look at its image and title, and ask whether the idea is clear at a glance, and whether the board it sits on matches the idea. A pin high on saves and low on outbound clicks is a pin that is kept but not followed: look at its description and its link, and ask whether the description gives a clear reason to click, and whether the linked page delivers what the pin promised. A pin high on outbound clicks is a pin that works for traffic: look at everything about it — image, title, description, board, page — and ask what the next pin in the same style could be. Write down the pattern, in words, not just in numbers: "pins with a numbered list in the title, a photograph of the finished result, and a link to a step-by-step page, saved to the board that matches the topic, earned the most outbound clicks." That sentence is a publishing brief for the next batch. A number on its own is not.

Group pins before you judge them. A single pin is an anecdote. A group of pins that share a feature — the same image style, the same title pattern, the same topic, the same board, the same linked page type — is a pattern. Compare groups, not singles: all the pins with text on the image against all the pins without it; all the pins that link to a tutorial against all the pins that link to a product page; all the pins on one board against all the pins on another. If one group is clearly ahead on the metric that matches your goal, publish more of that group, and change one feature at a time in the next batch so you can tell what helped. If no group is ahead, the difference may be the topic, not the style: a topic with strong search demand will outperform a topic with weak demand, however well the pin is made. That is a keyword and topic question, and the PinTrendFinder tool and the Trends guide are the tools for it. And remember the age effect: older pins have had more time to gather saves and clicks, so a new pin will look quiet beside a pin published six months ago. Compare pins of a similar age, or compare each pin's first month with the next pin's first month, rather than comparing a new pin's first week with an old pin's lifetime total. Age is the hidden variable in most pin comparisons, and it explains more apparent failures than any other factor.

5. Reading your boards: which collections earn attention, and which do not. Boards are collections, and on Pinterest a board is also a signal: a pin saved to a board whose title and description match the pin's topic is easier for Pinterest to place than the same pin on a vague board. Analytics can show you, board by board, which collections are earning impressions, saves, and clicks, and which are sitting quiet. Start with the boards that earn the most, and ask what they have in common: a plain keyword title, an honest description, a steady supply of fresh pins, a clear topic that matches the pins on it. Our guide to Pinterest board SEO explains the board setup in detail, and the analytics check is how you tell whether that setup is working. A board that earns attention is a board worth feeding: publish more pins to it, keep it filled, and give it the fresh images and the new angles on its topic. A board that earns nothing, over a month or more, needs a diagnosis before it needs more pins. Is the title a keyword phrase people search for, or a personal label only you understand? Is the description written in the words of the audience, or is it empty? Are there enough pins on it to look like a collection, or does it have three pins and look abandoned? Is the topic one people search for on Pinterest at all, or is it a topic better suited to another platform? Fix the board before you feed it. Publishing more pins onto a board with a vague title and an empty description will not fix the board, and it will make the eventual fix harder, because there will be more pins to move or retitle.

Board analytics also reveal a subtler pattern: the same pin, saved to two different boards, may perform differently on each. That is useful information, because it tells you which board's audience and topic match the pin best. When you publish a fresh pin, save it to the board that matches its topic most closely first, and note which board earned the response. If you save the same pin to several boards, do so sparingly and with a gap between saves, because flooding several boards with the same pin at once looks repetitive to a visitor and to Pinterest, and it muddies the record of which board earned what. Keep the board record in the same document as the pin record: pin, board, date, and the metrics after a month. Over time, that record will show you your strong boards and your weak ones, and the strong boards will become the core of your publishing plan. A focused account with a few strong boards, fed steadily, will outperform a scattered account with dozens of thin boards, and the analytics will prove it, board by board, if you keep the record.

6. Reading your audience: who is watching, and what to do with the answer. The audience view shows, in broad terms, who engages with your pins: the age ranges and genders Pinterest can infer, the places your audience is in, the devices they use, and the other topics and categories they are interested in. Read it as a broad sketch, not as a census. The numbers are estimates, based on the accounts Pinterest can identify, and they will not describe every person who sees a pin. The sketch is still useful, because it answers three practical questions. First, is the audience you are reaching the audience you intended? A home decor account whose audience is mostly interested in recipes has a topic problem, or a board problem, and the fix is in the topic and the boards, not in the pins. Second, what else is the audience interested in? The interests and categories in the audience view are a source of new topic ideas, within the account's main topic, because they show what the same people care about. A bedroom-storage audience that is also interested in small-space living is an audience that would welcome pins about small-space storage, and that is a topic expansion supported by evidence rather than by a guess. Third, what device does the audience use? If most of the audience is on a phone, as is common on Pinterest, the pin image must be readable at phone size, the title must be plain, and the linked page must work well on a phone. A page that loads slowly or reads badly on a phone will waste the clicks the pins earned, and no amount of pin analytics will fix a page problem.

Do not over-read the audience view. A small account's audience data will be thin, and a thin sample will swing from week to week. Do not change the account's topic because one week's audience sketch looked different from the last. Do not target a demographic in the abstract — "I will publish for this age group" — when the evidence is a broad estimate. And do not treat the audience view as a substitute for the pin view: the pins that earn saves and clicks are the evidence of what the audience wants, and the audience view is the context that helps you understand why. Use the audience view once a month, alongside the pin and board views, to check that the account is still reaching the people it is for, and to find one or two topic ideas for the next month. If the audience view shows a large share of your audience in a country or a language you had not considered, note it, and consider whether a board or a set of pins in that language, or for that market, would be worth testing — as a test, with a small batch, measured like any other change, not as a whole-account pivot on the strength of one view.

7. Connecting analytics with search: Trends, keywords, and the phrases behind the pins. Pinterest analytics shows what happened. Search and Trends show what people are looking for. The two together are much stronger than either alone, and the connection is the keyword: the phrase on the pin, in the title and description, is the phrase people searched for, and the analytics show whether the pin that carried that phrase was shown, saved, and clicked. When a pin performs well, note its keyword phrase, and check that phrase in the PinTrendFinder tool and in our Trends guide: is the phrase steady, rising, or seasonal? A steady phrase that performs well is a foundation: build more pins and more boards around it. A rising phrase that performs well is an opportunity: publish while the interest is rising, and keep the pins evergreen so they still work when the rise levels off. A seasonal phrase that performs well is a calendar item: note the season, publish early next year, and do not judge the topic by its off-season quiet. When a pin performs badly, check the phrase too: is it a phrase people actually search for, or a phrase only you would use? A pin with the wrong phrase will not be found, however good the image is, and the fix is a keyword fix, not a design fix. Our pin descriptions guide explains how to write the title and description around the phrase, for the reader first and for search second, and the analytics check is how you tell whether the phrase you chose was the right one.

Keep a keyword record alongside the pin record: phrase, pin, board, date, and the metrics after a month. Over a few months, that record becomes a list of phrases that work for your account, and a list of phrases that do not. The working list is the raw material for the next keyword research: find related phrases, longer phrases, and neighbouring topics around the phrases that work, using Pinterest search suggestions and the PinTrendFinder tool, and publish fresh pins for those phrases. The non-working list is equally valuable: it tells you which phrases to stop using, or to use differently — a different image, a different board, a different page — rather than repeating the same pin and hoping for a different result. Do not treat Trends data as a promise. A rising trend does not guarantee that your pin will be shown, saved, or clicked, and a steady phrase does not guarantee traffic. Trends show interest. Analytics show response. The two together show opportunity. The pin, the board, and the page decide whether the opportunity is taken.

8. Turning stats into action: the weekly decision, step by step. A weekly analytics check should end with a decision, not just a look. Work through the steps in order, write the decision down, and carry it into the next publishing batch. Step 1: Note the direction. Is the account up, down, or flat on the metrics that match your goal — outbound clicks for traffic, saves for distribution, follows for audience — compared with the previous period of the same length? Step 2: Find the cause. Which pins, boards, or topics moved? Look at the top pins for each metric, and at the boards that earned attention, and match the movement to what you published, and when. If nothing moved, the cause may be seasonal, or the account may have published too little to move. Step 3: Write the pattern in one sentence. "Pins with a numbered title, a photograph of the finished result, and a link to a step-by-step page, on the matching board, earned the most outbound clicks." If you cannot write the sentence, you have not found a pattern yet, and you should publish another batch before changing anything. Step 4: Choose one change. One change, not five: a new title style, a new image style, a new board for a topic, a new topic from the audience or Trends view, or a new page type to link to. Step 5: Publish the test batch. A small batch — five to ten fresh pins — that carries the change, saved to the right boards, with the record kept. Step 6: Wait, then check. Give the batch a month, check the same metrics for the same pins, and compare with the previous batch. If the change helped, keep it. If it did not, revert it, and test the next change. That loop — note, find, write, choose, publish, wait, check — is the whole method. It is slow, and it is the only method that works, because it is the only method that separates a real pattern from a lucky week.

The actions that follow from the loop are usually ordinary, and that is a good sign. Publish more of the topic that earned saves and clicks. Give a good page a fresh image and a fresh title, and publish it as a new pin. Move a pin to the board that matches it best, and save future pins on that topic to that board. Rewrite a title that is clever but unclear into a title that names the benefit plainly, following the pin descriptions guide. Fix a page that earns clicks but loses visitors, by making the page load faster, read better on a phone, and deliver what the pin promised. Set up Rich Pins if the site is claimed and the pages carry the metadata, so that every future pin from the site is more informative. Try an Idea Pin that shows the making of something, if the account's standard pins are routine, and measure it by saves and follows rather than by outbound clicks, because an Idea Pin carries no link. Each action is small. Each action is measured. Together, over months, they are how an account improves. 9. Experiments that teach: changing one thing at a time. An experiment is a question with a record. The question should be specific: "Do pins with the number in the title earn more outbound clicks than pins with the same image and a title without a number?" The record should be complete: the pins, the boards, the dates, and the metrics after a month. The change should be one thing: the title, the image, the board, the description, or the page — never two at once, because two changes at once cannot be told apart. Run the experiment on a small batch, not on the whole account, so that a failed test costs a week, not a month. Compare the test batch with a control batch — pins of the same topic, published in the same period, without the change — so that the season, the topic, and the account's direction do not confuse the result. And decide in advance what counts as a result: a clear difference on the metric that matches your goal, sustained over the month, not a single day's spike. If the test batch is ahead on outbound clicks, keep the change. If it is behind, drop it. If there is no clear difference, the change did not matter, and you can spend the next test on something else. Most tests will show no clear difference, and that is normal. The value of testing is not that every test succeeds. It is that the account stops repeating the same guess, and starts building a record of what actually worked, for this account, with this audience, on these topics.

Good test subjects, in order of usefulness, are: the title style, the image style, the board placement, the topic, and the linked page. The title and the image are the first impression, and they are the cheapest to test, because a fresh image and a fresh title make a new pin from an existing page. The board is the placement, and it is worth testing when a pin performs well on one board and quietly on another. The topic is the biggest decision, and it should be tested with a small batch — five to ten pins — before it becomes a board or a publishing commitment. The linked page is the destination, and it is worth testing when pins earn clicks but the site earns little from them: a clearer page, a faster page, or a page that matches the pin more closely may do more than any number of new pins. Keep the test record in the same document as the pin record, with the question, the change, the batch, and the result. Over a year, that record will be the most valuable document the account owns, because it will show, in the account's own numbers, what works and what does not, without relying on anyone else's advice, including this guide's.

10. Misreadings that waste time — and how to avoid them. The most common misreading is judging a pin too soon. A pin needs time to be shown, saved, and clicked, and a judgement made after two days is a judgement about two days, not about the pin. Wait a month before you call a pin a failure, and wait a month before you call a pin a success. The second misreading is comparing unlike with unlike: a new pin with an old pin, a seasonal pin with an evergreen pin, a pin on a strong board with a pin on a weak board, a pin that links to a tutorial with a pin that links to a shop page. Compare like with like, or do not compare at all. The third misreading is chasing impressions. Impressions are distribution, not interest, and a pin with many impressions and few clicks is not a successful pin. It is a pin that was shown widely and chosen rarely. The fix is in the pin or the board, not in more publishing. The fourth misreading is ignoring the page. A pin that earns outbound clicks but links to a slow, confusing, or irrelevant page will not bring value, and the analytics will show the clicks while the site shows the bounces. Read the two analytics together. The fifth misreading is changing everything at once. A new title style, a new image style, new boards, and a new topic, all in one week, cannot be measured, and the account will not know what helped or what harmed it. Change one thing, measure it, and then change the next. The sixth misreading is treating analytics as a verdict on the account. Analytics is a record of the last period's publishing. A quiet month is not a failed account. It is a record that says: publish, check the setup, and publish again. The checklist is the tool for the setup check, and the weekly loop in this guide is the tool for the publishing check. Use both, and do not let a single quiet week change the plan.

11. A monthly routine, and the limits of what analytics can say. Once a month, do a longer review, in addition to the weekly check. Compare the month with the previous month, and with the same month last year if the account is old enough. Note the top five pins for each metric, the strongest and weakest boards, the audience sketch, and the keyword phrases that worked. Write three decisions for the next month: one topic to publish more of, one change to test, and one fix to make — a board to retitle, a page to improve, a setup item from the checklist to complete. Write the decisions down, and check them at the next monthly review. A monthly review that ends with three decisions is a useful meeting with yourself. A monthly review that ends with a look at a line and a feeling is not. Keep the monthly record with the weekly record, in the same document, so that the year builds into a history the account can learn from. And keep the record honest: note the failures as well as the successes, the tests that showed nothing, and the months when publishing slipped. A record that only shows successes is a record that cannot teach.

Finally, know the limits. Pinterest analytics shows what happened on Pinterest, for the pins and boards it can attribute, over the period you chose, with some delay. It does not show every view, every save, or every click with perfect accuracy, and it does not show what happened after a person left Pinterest, except in the broad terms your own site's analytics can add. It cannot tell you why a person saved or clicked, only that they did. It cannot promise that a pattern will repeat, because the audience, the season, and Pinterest itself change. And it cannot replace judgement: a pin that earns little but serves a small, loyal audience may be worth keeping, and a pin that earns much but misleads its clickers is not worth repeating, however good the numbers look. Read the numbers, write the pattern, test the change, and keep the account honest — clear pins, honest descriptions, pages that deliver what the pin promised, and a steady rhythm. That is what Pinterest analytics is for: not a score, but a record, read slowly, acted on patiently, and kept over months. Start with the next weekly check, and let the record do the quiet work of making the next batch better than the last.
