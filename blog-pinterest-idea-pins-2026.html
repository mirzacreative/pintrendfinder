Pinterest Idea Pins in 2026: How to Use Them

A practical guide to Pinterest Idea Pins in 2026: what they are, how they differ from standard pins, how to make one step by step, what makes them perform — and how to fit them into a Pinterest SEO workflow that actually brings traffic.

Quick answer: An Idea Pin is a multi-page, story-style pin made of video or images that stays on your profile and is built for saves and follows, not clicks — it carries no outbound link. Use Idea Pins to show a process, a tutorial, or a before-and-after in a few short pages, give each one a keyword-rich title and description, save it to a matching board, and pair it with a standard pin on the same topic that links to your site. That pairing is the whole strategy: the Idea Pin builds the audience, the standard pin collects the traffic.

Most Pinterest creators either ignore Idea Pins completely or post them at random and wonder why nothing happens. Both mistakes come from the same misunderstanding: treating an Idea Pin like a normal pin. A standard pin is a doorway — one image, one link, one click out to your site. An Idea Pin is a room. The viewer stays inside Pinterest, taps through several pages, watches a short video, and decides whether you are worth following. That is a different job, and it needs a different plan. This guide explains what Idea Pins are, how they differ from standard pins, how to make one from scratch, what tends to perform, and how to connect them to your keyword and board strategy so the work you put into them keeps paying off. One honest warning first: Pinterest changes its features often. Buttons move, limits change, and formats get renamed. The ideas in this guide will hold up, but the exact steps and options in the app should always be checked in the app itself on the day you publish.

1. What an Idea Pin actually is. An Idea Pin is a pin made of more than one page. Each page can be a short video clip or a still image, and the viewer taps through the pages in order, like a short story. Idea Pins live on your profile in their own section, and they stay there — unlike the short-lived stories on other platforms, they do not disappear after a day. That permanence is the point. An Idea Pin is meant to be found weeks or months later by someone searching for its topic, in the same way a standard pin is. The format suits anything that takes more than one step to explain: a recipe with stages, a room makeover with before and after shots, a tutorial with three or four moves, a list of tips where each tip gets its own page, or a product shown from several angles. If your content can be understood from a single image, a standard pin is the better format. If it needs a sequence — first this, then this, then the result — that is what Idea Pins are for. Think of them as the show-and-tell version of your content, while standard pins are the signpost version.

2. How Idea Pins differ from standard pins. The differences matter more than the similarities, so here they are plainly. A standard pin has one image and one outbound link: someone clicks it and leaves Pinterest for your website, your shop, or your blog. An Idea Pin has no outbound link. Nobody clicks through to your site from it, and that is by design. A standard pin is built for clicks; an Idea Pin is built for saves, follows, and watch time inside Pinterest. A standard pin is a single image, so its whole message has to fit in one frame. An Idea Pin spreads its message across several pages, so it can teach, demonstrate, or tell a short story. A standard pin ranks in search and brings traffic for months. An Idea Pin can also be discovered in search, but its main value is different: it shows a viewer what you know and why they should follow you, and it keeps working on your profile as a sample of your best work. The practical consequence is simple. Never judge an Idea Pin by the clicks it brings, because it brings none. Judge it by saves, by follows, and by whether the people who watch it go on to save your standard pins. And never publish an Idea Pin as your only pin on a topic — always make the matching standard pin that links out, so the interest the Idea Pin creates has somewhere to go.

3. Why Idea Pins are worth your time. If Idea Pins bring no clicks, why make them at all? Because clicks are only the last step of the journey, and most Pinterest users are not ready to click the first time they see you. They save first, follow second, and click third — often weeks later, when they are ready to act on the idea they saved. Idea Pins work on the first two steps. A viewer who watches a short tutorial to the end has learned something from you, and that is a stronger reason to follow you than a single pretty image. A viewer who saves your Idea Pin has told Pinterest they want more like it, and Pinterest remembers that. Idea Pins also give your profile depth. A profile with only standard pins looks like a catalogue. A profile with a row of Idea Pins looks like a creator who teaches, demonstrates, and has something to say. That matters when someone lands on your profile and decides in seconds whether to follow. There is a second, quieter benefit: making an Idea Pin forces you to organise your content. Turning a blog post into five clear pages shows you whether the post actually has five clear points — and if it does not, you have learned something about the post. None of this means Idea Pins should replace standard pins. They should sit beside them, doing the job standard pins cannot do: turning a stranger into a follower before that follower ever visits your site.

4. Before you make one: pick a topic people actually search for. The most common Idea Pin mistake is choosing a topic because you like it, not because anyone is searching for it. An Idea Pin about a topic nobody searches for is a performance nobody attends. Start with keyword research, exactly as you would for a standard pin. Open Pinterest search and type a word from your niche, then read the suggestions that appear — those are real searches, and each one is a candidate topic. Compare two or three candidates in Pinterest Trends to see which ones hold steady interest and which are fading, using our guide on how to use Pinterest Trends to find keywords. Then cross-check the exact phrases with the free PinTrendFinder tool, so the topic you choose is backed by more than a guess. Good Idea Pin topics share three traits. They are specific enough to finish in a few pages — "five ways to fold a fitted sheet" works, "home organisation" does not. They have a visible result — a finished dish, a styled shelf, a solved problem — because Idea Pins are a visual format and a visible result is what makes someone tap to the last page. And they match something you already have: a blog post, a product, or a standard pin that the Idea Pin can point people towards. If you cannot name the standard pin or post that pairs with your Idea Pin topic, pick a different topic.

5. How to make an Idea Pin, step by step. Making your first Idea Pin takes longer than making a standard pin, so set aside an hour and follow these steps in order. First, write the outline on paper before you open the app: one line per page, no more than five or six pages, each line a single point. A recipe outline might read: the finished dish, the ingredients laid out, the key step, the common mistake, the serving suggestion. Second, gather or shoot the visuals for each page. Short vertical video clips work best for steps that move — stirring, folding, painting, assembling — while still images work best for lists, quotes, and before-and-after comparisons. Film in good daylight, hold the phone steady, and keep the subject large in the frame; Pinterest is mostly viewed on phones, and small details disappear. Third, open Pinterest, start a new Idea Pin, and add your pages in order. Fourth, add text overlays to each page: a few words that say what the page is about, large enough to read on a phone screen without zooming. Fifth, add music if the app offers a track that fits the mood — quiet and unobtrusive beats loud and distracting. Sixth, write the title and description using the keyword you researched, exactly as you would for a standard pin. Seventh, choose the board it belongs on, publish, and then immediately create the matching standard pin that links to your site. Check each step in the app as you go, because Pinterest rearranges these screens from time to time and the button names may have changed since this guide was written.

6. The hook: the first page decides everything. The first page of your Idea Pin is the only page most viewers will ever see, so it has to do three jobs at once: show the result, name the topic, and create a reason to tap forward. Show the result first. If your Idea Pin is a recipe, the first page should be the finished dish, not the raw ingredients. If it is a makeover, the first page should be the finished room, not the empty one. People tap forward to find out how something was done — they have to see the something first. Name the topic in plain words on that first page, using the keyword people search for: "5-Minute Weeknight Pasta" tells a viewer exactly what they will get, while "Dinner Tonight" tells them nothing. Then add the reason to keep watching: a promise the rest of the pin will deliver. "The mistake that makes it soggy — page 4" gives a viewer a reason to tap through three more pages. "Swipe for the exact measurements" does the same job in fewer words. What does not work as a hook is a title card with no image, a page that only says your brand name, or a slow introduction that explains who you are before showing anything useful. Nobody came to meet you; they came for the result. Introduce yourself after you have earned their attention, not before.

7. Length, pacing, and page count. Shorter beats longer, almost every time. A viewer who finishes your Idea Pin has had a complete experience with you; a viewer who abandons it halfway has had an interrupted one, and is less likely to follow. Aim for three to six pages, with each page carrying exactly one point. One tip per page, one step per page, one before-and-after per page. If a page carries two points, split it. If a page carries no point at all, delete it. Keep each video page short — a few seconds of clear action is worth more than a long clip that repeats itself. Keep the pace steady: pages should move at roughly the same speed, with no single page that drags while the others rush. A useful test is to watch your own Idea Pin as a stranger would, with the sound off, tapping through at normal speed. If you get bored, your audience will too. If a page confuses you, it will confuse them. Cut ruthlessly. Five strong pages will always outperform eight pages where three are filler, because the viewer who reaches the last page of a five-page pin is a viewer who has seen everything you wanted to show them — and is ready for the call to action on the final page.

8. Text overlays, music, and calls to action. Text overlays are what make an Idea Pin work with the sound off, and many Pinterest users browse with the sound off, so treat the overlays as the main message, not decoration. Keep each overlay to a few words — a short phrase, not a sentence. Put the words where they can be read: high contrast against the image behind them, large enough for a phone screen, and clear of the edges where the app's own buttons sit. Use the same style of overlay on every page so the pin looks like one piece of work, not five unrelated images. Music should support the mood and then get out of the way. A calm track suits a home or recipe pin; an upbeat track suits a fashion or travel pin. If no track fits, silence is better than a wrong track, and a pin with clear text overlays works perfectly well without music at all. End with a call to action, but make it one that fits a format with no link. "Save this for your next dinner party" asks for a save, which is exactly what an Idea Pin can deliver. "Follow for more small-space ideas" asks for a follow, which is the other thing it can deliver. "Full recipe on my blog — see my pins" points the viewer to the standard pin that links out. What does not work is "click the link" — there is no link to click, and asking for one only confuses the viewer. Match the ask to what the format can actually do.

9. Titles, descriptions, and boards: the SEO layer. An Idea Pin still needs the same keyword care as a standard pin, because Pinterest still has to understand what it is about. Write the title with your main keyword near the front, in the words people actually type: "Small Bedroom Storage Ideas That Actually Work" is a title Pinterest can match to searches; "My Favourite Room" is not. Write the description in one or two plain sentences that use the main keyword plus two or three related phrases, naturally woven in — the same approach as a pin description. For the pin-level detail, read our guide to Pinterest pin descriptions that rank. Then choose the board carefully. Save the Idea Pin to the board whose topic matches it most closely, because the board tells Pinterest what the pin is about — our guide to Pinterest board SEO explains how that works. A recipe Idea Pin belongs on your recipe board, not on a general board about everything, and it should never be saved to a board whose topic it does not match. If you have no board that fits the Idea Pin, that is a sign the topic does not fit your account — either create the board first, with a keyword title and a proper description, or choose a topic that fits a board you already have. The board is part of the ranking, and an Idea Pin on the wrong board is an Idea Pin Pinterest cannot place.

10. What to do after publishing — and the mistakes to avoid. Publishing is the start, not the end. In the days after an Idea Pin goes live, check its saves and watch-through in your analytics: which pages do viewers reach, and where do they stop? If viewers leave after page two, your hook promised something page three did not deliver. If they watch to the end but do not save, your final page asked for the wrong thing — or asked for nothing at all. Use what you learn on the next Idea Pin, not by editing the old one endlessly. And make the matching standard pin the same day, while the topic is fresh, so the traffic has somewhere to go. The mistakes to avoid are the ones this guide keeps coming back to: choosing a topic nobody searches for; opening with a title card instead of the result; stuffing ten pages where five would do; writing overlays so small nobody can read them; asking viewers to click a link that does not exist; and publishing Idea Pins with no standard pin to catch the interest they create. One more mistake is subtler: treating Idea Pins as a separate hobby from the rest of your account. They are not separate. The keyword you researched, the board you saved to, the standard pin that links out — those are one workflow, and the Idea Pin is the part of it that turns a searcher into a follower. Do the workflow whole, or do not do it at all.

Start with one. Pick a blog post or a standard pin that already performs, take its three best points, and turn them into a three-page Idea Pin this week: the result on page one, the key step on page two, the call to save on page three. Give it a keyword title, a plain description, and the right board, then make the matching standard pin the same day. That is one hour of work, and it is the cheapest way to learn what your audience actually watches to the end. If the first one works, make it a habit — one Idea Pin a week, always paired with a standard pin, always on a topic people search for. The Idea Pin builds the audience; the standard pin brings the traffic. Together, they are the whole strategy.
